Two staples of audio-equipment characterisation, from a captured signal: how much
an amplifier or transducer **distorts** a test tone, and what **frequency
response** an input/output measurement reveals. This page covers the IEC 60268-3
distortion set: total and nth-order harmonic distortion, THD+N and SINAD
through the AES17 measurement bandwidth, the per-order modulation and
difference-frequency intermodulation, dynamic intermodulation (DIM) and the
ITU-R 468 weighted THD, and the Bendat & Piersol frequency-response estimators
$H_1$/$H_2$ with the ordinary coherence $\gamma^2$. Every quantity has an exact analytic
oracle (synthetic signals with known harmonic and intermodulation amplitudes, a
clipped-sine Fourier oracle, a full DIM test-signal synthesis, and a known LTI
path), so the numbers are verifiable rather than tuned.

Everything here is measured on a captured signal. What a transducer datasheet
then makes of those curves is the subject of the two type-test guides,
[Loudspeaker Characterisation (IEC 60268-5)](/phonometry/devices/electroacoustics/loudspeakers/)
and [Microphone Characterisation (IEC 60268-4)](/phonometry/devices/electroacoustics/microphones/),
which own the sensitivity conventions, the polar patterns and the
rated-characteristics fiches. The one-sweep alternative to the steady-tone
tests of sections 1 and 2, with THD as a continuous function of the excitation
frequency, is
[Swept-sine distortion](/phonometry/devices/electroacoustics/swept-sine-distortion/).

## 0. What is captured, and under what conditions

Every ratio on this page is a property of a device **at an operating point**,
not of the device, so IEC 60268-3 defines them all under one set of conditions
and the numbers are meaningless without it.

**The bench.** Clause 3.1.2 puts the amplifier under *rated conditions*: the
rated power supply; the source e.m.f. applied through the **rated source
impedance**; the output terminals loaded with the **rated load impedance** — a
non-inductive power resistor, not a loudspeaker, so the load is known and the
measurement repeatable; the source a sinusoid at the 1 kHz standard reference
frequency of IEC 60268-1; the volume control set so that the rated
distortion-limited output voltage appears; the tone controls flat and the
balance control central. Everything after the load is instrumentation: an
attenuator or divider down to the analyser, with at least 10 dB of converter
headroom so the amplifier and never the converter is the element that clips.

**The operating point.** *Standard measuring conditions* (clause 3.1.3) are
rated conditions with the source e.m.f. dropped by 10 dB. The clause's own
reason (14.12.3.1, NOTE 1) is that a well-designed system spends most of its
time about 10 dB below the rated distortion-limited output, because that is
where the amplitude distribution of speech and music sits. A THD measured at
full rated output and one measured at standard measuring conditions are
therefore different numbers for the same amplifier, and a datasheet figure
quoted without its level cannot be compared with either.

**Two conditions that are easy to skip.** A Class D output must pass the
analogue low-pass filter of IEC 61606-1 between the load and the analyser
(clause 3.1.2, NOTE 2, Figure 2), or the switching residual is counted as noise
— and the AES17 20 kHz digital band-limit of section 2 cannot remove a residual
that has already aliased inside the converter. And clause 9 requires the
amplifier to be run under approximately standard measuring conditions for 1 h
before the first reading, because internal heating moves the output voltage.

**What has to be recorded.** None of the ratios below means anything on its
own. Report, with every figure: the fundamental frequency, the output voltage
or power (or the sound pressure level, for an acoustic measurement), the load
impedance, the measurement bandwidth and any weighting, and the number of
harmonic orders summed. `thd` and `harmonic_analysis` default to
`n_harmonics=10`; an analyser summing five orders reads lower on the same
signal, so the order count belongs beside the number.

## 1. Harmonic distortion (IEC 60268-3 14.12.2–5)

A non-linear device fed a pure sine at $f_1$ returns the fundamental plus
harmonics at $2f_1, 3f_1, \dots$. The **total harmonic distortion** combines the
harmonic amplitudes $a_n$, either relative to the fundamental (`kind='F'`) or to
the total RMS (`kind='R'`):

$$
\mathrm{THD}_F = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_{n\ge 2} a_n^2}}{a_1}, \qquad
\mathrm{THD}_R = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_{n\ge 2} a_n^2}}{\sqrt{\sum_{n\ge 1} a_n^2}},
\qquad
d_n = \frac{a_n}{\sqrt{\sum_{k\ge 1} a_k^2}}.
$$

$d_n$ is the nth-order harmonic distortion (the nth harmonic relative to the
total). The tones should fall on FFT bins: use coherent sampling (an integer
number of periods) or a low-leakage window so the amplitudes are read without
spectral leakage.

Only the harmonics that fall **below Nyquist** are summed — the implementation
stops the sum at the first order that reaches $f_\mathrm{s}/2$ — so the effective order
count shrinks as the test frequency rises: at 48 kHz a 1 kHz test sums ten
orders and a 7 kHz test sums two, and a THD sweep at fixed `n_harmonics` is not
measuring the same quantity at both ends. Sample at 96 kHz or higher when
testing above about 2 kHz. In the recording itself the converter folds any real
distortion product generated above its own Nyquist frequency back into the
band, where it lands at an unrelated frequency: THD+N counts it and the
harmonic sum does not, which is one reason the two can disagree by more than
the noise floor explains.

Distortion is a property of the device *at a level*, which is why IEC 60268-3
quotes every figure with its frequency and its input or output level, and why a
curve of THD against level says more than any single number: an amplifier is
cleanest at a few watts and folds over near clipping, while a loudspeaker's THD
grows monotonically with excursion. The orders of magnitude are worth
carrying — below 0.01 % for a competent power amplifier at rated output, 0.1 to
1 % for a converter chain, 1 to 3 % for a direct radiator through its mid band
and around 10 % where it reaches its excursion limit — because they mean a
"2 %" reading condemns an amplifier and is unremarkable for a woofer.

<ThemeImage src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jmrplens/phonometry/main/.github/images/distortion.svg" alt="Magnitude spectrum of a 1 kHz single-tone test in dB relative to the fundamental, with the first five harmonics marked above a broadband noise floor, annotated with THD (F), THD (R), THD+N and SINAD" width="82%" loading="eager" />

*The harmonic ladder is the device's signature, and its shape is diagnostic: a
dominant second harmonic points at an asymmetric non-linearity (a biased stage,
an off-centre voice coil), a dominant third at symmetric compression (clipping,
a suspension reaching its limit). Each marker reads directly as a percentage,
since $-34$ dB relative to the fundamental is 2 %. The four annotated numbers
are one capture read four ways: $\mathrm{THD}_F$ counts the harmonics against
the fundamental, $\mathrm{THD}_R$ against the total — they differ by the factor
$1/\sqrt{1 + \mathrm{THD}_F^2}$ and are indistinguishable below about 10 %,
which is why the two annotations sit on top of each other here — while THD+N
adds the grass between the harmonics, and SINAD is its reciprocal in dB.*

```python
from phonometry import electroacoustics

thd_f = electroacoustics.thd(signal, fs, 1000.0, kind="F")          # relative to fundamental
thd_r = electroacoustics.thd(signal, fs, 1000.0, kind="R")          # relative to total RMS
d2 = electroacoustics.harmonic_distortion(  # 2nd-order harmonic
    signal, fs, fundamental=1000.0, order=2
)
```

`harmonic_analysis` bundles the fundamental, the harmonic amplitudes and the THD
(both conventions), THD+N and SINAD into one plottable result:

```python
from phonometry import electroacoustics

res = electroacoustics.harmonic_analysis(signal, fs, 1000.0)
print(res.thd_f, res.thd_r, res.thd_plus_noise, res.sinad_db)
res.plot()   # annotated harmonic spectrum (needs matplotlib)
```

:::caution
**Three checks before you believe a distortion number.**

- *Source purity* (14.12.3.2 a). The total harmonic distortion of the signal
  source must be at least 10 dB below the lowest distortion to be measured. A
  soundcard generator often fails that, so measure the generator straight into
  the analyser first and treat the result as the floor of the bench.
- *Noise residual* (14.12.3.2 d). With the source e.m.f. reduced to zero, the
  residual output must be **less than one third** of the measured distortion
  voltage; if it is not, the clause says the measurement is being affected by
  noise and the results shall be discarded. The fallback it names is the
  per-order measurement of 14.12.5, which is what `harmonic_distortion` gives.
- *Band ceiling* (14.12.4.1). If the harmonics that matter fall above the upper
  limit of the amplifier or of the analyser, the measured THD is misleadingly
  low. The highest valid fundamental is $f_\text{limit}/n$ for the highest
  significant order $n$ — the clause's own case is a 30 kHz limit with a
  significant fifth harmonic, giving 6 kHz, or 10 kHz if the third is the
  highest that matters. A THD sweep taken to 10 kHz at 48 kHz sampling falls
  for that reason and not because the device improved.

:::

The residual test is two lines, and the clause makes it mandatory rather than
advisory:

```python
# `electroacoustics` as imported above. Capture `idle` the same length as
# `signal`, with the generator muted, and run the identical call on it.
residual = electroacoustics.thd(idle, fs, 1000.0, kind="F")
print(residual < thd_f / 3.0)   # False -> discard, and drop to harmonic_distortion
```

## 2. THD+N and SINAD (AES17-2015 6.3)

Where THD counts only the harmonics, **THD+N** compares *everything but the
fundamental* (harmonics **and** noise) with the total signal. AES17 removes the
fundamental with a standard notch filter ($1.2 \le Q \le 3$, validated on the
*applied* zero-phase response per clause 5.2.8) and takes the ratio of the
residual to the total RMS; **SINAD** is its reciprocal in dB:

$$
\mathrm{THD{+}N} = \frac{V_\text{residual}}{V_\text{total}}, \qquad
\mathrm{SINAD} = -20\log_{10}(\mathrm{THD{+}N})\ \mathrm{dB}.
$$

Both voltages are measured through the AES17 measurement bandwidth (a 20 Hz
high-pass plus the standard low-pass at 20 kHz, clauses 5.2.5 and 6.3.1) so a
DC offset or ultrasonic noise at a high sample rate does not count as "noise".
The band edge is configurable, and `bandwidth=None` disables the chain and
measures the full Nyquist band.

```python
from phonometry import electroacoustics

ratio = electroacoustics.thd_plus_noise(signal, fs, 1000.0)          # ratio (0..)
db = electroacoustics.thd_plus_noise(signal, fs, 1000.0, as_db=True)  # 20·lg(ratio) dB
sinad_db = electroacoustics.sinad(signal, fs, 1000.0)                 # = -db
wide = electroacoustics.thd_plus_noise(signal, fs, 1000.0, bandwidth=None)  # full Nyquist
```

Because THD+N counts the in-band noise floor, it is at or above the
harmonic-only THD; `SINAD` is the corresponding signal-to-noise-and-distortion
headroom in dB (a quantity derived from the AES17 THD+N; AES17 itself does not
define SINAD). The notch discards a start/stop transient internally, so the
measurement wants a steady, sufficiently long capture.

### 2.1 Weighted THD (IEC 60268-3 14.12.11)

`weighted_thd` frequency-weights the notched residual before taking the ratio,
so the perceptual emphasis of the distortion products is accounted for. The
default weighting is the network the clause requires: the IEC 60268-1
Appendix A curve, i.e. ITU-R BS.468-4, which peaks at +12.2 dB near 6.3 kHz
(exposed as `itu_r_468_weighting`); `'A'` and `'C'` remain as labelled options.
Per the clause, the weighted measurement is valid for fundamental frequencies
between 31.5 Hz and 400 Hz:

```python
from phonometry import electroacoustics

print(electroacoustics.weighted_thd(signal, fs, 100.0))                  # ITU-R 468 network
print(electroacoustics.weighted_thd(signal, fs, 100.0, weighting="A"))   # A-weighted variant
print(electroacoustics.itu_r_468_weighting([6300.0]))                    # [+12.2] dB
```

<ThemeImage src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jmrplens/phonometry/main/.github/images/itu_r_468_weighting.svg" alt="The ITU-R BS.468-4 weighting network against frequency, rising to plus 12.2 dB near 6.3 kHz and falling steeply above it, with the CCIR-RMS variant shifted down by 5.63 dB so that it is 0 dB at 2 kHz, and the A-weighting curve drawn pale as a reference; the marked points are 0 dB at 1 kHz and plus 12.2 dB at 6.3 kHz for the 468 form and minus 5.63 dB at 1 kHz for CCIR-RMS" width="82%" />

*One curve governs three quantities on this page and a fourth on the microphone
guide, so it is worth reading once. It is not a loudness contour: it is steeper
than A-weighting on both flanks and peaks two octaves higher. For the 100 Hz
fundamental of the snippet it discounts the second harmonic by 13.8 dB and the
third by 10.3 dB and lifts everything above 1 kHz — the tenth order and up — so
a weighted THD is not a "more accurate" THD but a deliberate re-ranking: it
forgives the low-order distortion of a soft non-linearity and penalises the
high-order products a crossover or a slew-rate limit throws off. That is why
the clause restricts the measurement to fundamentals between 31.5 Hz and
400 Hz, where the orders that matter land under the peak. The CCIR-RMS form is
the same curve moved down so that 2 kHz rather than 1 kHz is the unity point —
a renormalisation, not a different network.*

## 3. Dynamic range and idle channel noise (AES17-2015 6.4)

The two catalogue figures of any converter reuse the notch of section 2 and the
ITU-R 468 curve of section 2.1, but the excitation is different, which is why
clause 6.4 is a sibling of the THD+N clause and not a child of it. Both share
the AES17 **CCIR-RMS** weighting (5.2.7): the ITU-R BS.468-4 curve shifted by a
flat -5.63 dB so it is unity at 2 kHz. **Dynamic range** (6.4.1) drives the
device with a 997 Hz sine 60 dB below full scale, removes the fundamental with
the standard notch (5.2.8) and weights the residual noise-plus-distortion, then
reports the ratio of the full-scale sine to that weighted residual (also known
as the signal-to-noise ratio). **Idle channel noise** (6.4.2) is the same
weighted level measured with the device driven by digital zero, reported
relative to full scale:

```python
from phonometry import electroacoustics

# Dynamic range: capture the output of the -60 dBFS 997 Hz test, scaled so 1.0
# is digital full scale.
dr = electroacoustics.dynamic_range(output, fs, 997.0)     # dB CCIR-RMS
# Idle channel noise: capture the output with a digital-zero input.
idle = electroacoustics.idle_channel_noise(idle_output, fs)  # dBFS CCIR-RMS
```

Both are measured through the AES17 band (a 20 Hz high-pass plus the standard
low-pass at `bandwidth`). Because the CCIR-RMS filter reads -5.63 dB at 1 kHz,
a 1 kHz tone measures its own dBFS minus 5.63 dB, which pins the weighting
exactly.

Neither constant in the test is arbitrary. 997 Hz is a prime number, so it is
incommensurate with 44.1 kHz and 48 kHz alike: the tone never repeats over a
short record and walks the converter's whole code space, where a 1000 Hz tone
at 48 kHz would revisit the same 48 samples for ever and hide the
differential-non-linearity errors of the codes it never touches. The -60 dBFS
level keeps the converter well clear of its own overload region, where its
distortion rather than its noise would dominate, while still giving the notch a
fundamental to remove — which is why the result is referred to full scale and
not to the test tone.

Read the output on this scale. A correctly dithered 16-bit path is bounded at
about 93 dB unweighted ($6.02 \times 16 + 1.76 - 4.77$ dB for triangular
dither), and CCIR-RMS costs a further 1.6 dB on a flat noise floor, so anything
near 92 dB is 16 bits working perfectly and nothing better is available from
that word length; a modern 24-bit converter is quoted at 110 dB and upward.
Idle channel noise usually comes out a few decibels better than the dynamic
range for the same device, because it carries no distortion at all. Both are
weighted figures, so an unweighted noise specification from another datasheet
reads several decibels worse for the same hardware and the two must not be
compared.

## 4. Intermodulation distortion (IEC 60268-3 14.12.7–10)

When two tones pass through a non-linearity they beat against each other,
producing sum and difference products. IEC 60268-3 standardises four two-tone
tests, each with its own per-order definition; the last is a fixed-tone variant
of the second, and the bullets run in clause order:

- **Modulation distortion** (14.12.7): a large low tone $f_1$ and a small high
  tone $f_2$, whose *amplitude* ratio — not a frequency ratio — is preferably
  4:1, which the clause realises by setting the $f_1$ output 12 dB and the
  $f_2$ output 24 dB below the rated output voltage. The frequencies are
  chosen far apart (60 Hz and 7 kHz here) so the sidebands are resolvable and
  $f_2 - 2f_1$ stays above the highest significant harmonic of $f_1$. The
  per-order values are *arithmetic* sums of the sideband amplitudes relative to
  the $f_2$ output: $d_{\mathrm{m},2} = (a_{f_2+f_1} + a_{f_2-f_1})/a_{f_2}$ and
  $d_{\mathrm{m},3} = (a_{f_2+2f_1} + a_{f_2-2f_1})/a_{f_2}$. The result also carries the
  combined-RMS `smpte` value that SMPTE-type analyzers report (not an IEC
  quantity).
- **Difference-frequency distortion** (14.12.8): two equal high tones
  $f_1 < f_2$, referenced to $U_{2,\text{ref}} = 2U_{2,f_2}$ (the sum of both
  tone amplitudes): $d_{\mathrm{d},2} = a_{f_2-f_1}/(a_{f_1}+a_{f_2})$ and the arithmetic
  $d_{\mathrm{d},3} = (a_{2f_2-f_1} + a_{2f_1-f_2})/(a_{f_1}+a_{f_2})$.
- **Dynamic intermodulation** (DIM, 14.12.9): a 15 kHz sine plus a
  low-pass-filtered 3.15 kHz square wave (1:4 peak-to-peak). The DIM is the
  RMS of the intermodulation products $|k\,f_\text{square} \pm f_\text{sine}|$
  that fall below $f_\text{sine}$ (IEC 60268-3 Table 2), relative to the 15 kHz
  sine amplitude
  (the 14.12.9.1 definition; the 14.12.9.2 f) print of the denominator is an
  editorial defect, see the [errata registry](/phonometry/reference/errata/)).
- **Total difference-frequency distortion** (14.12.10): the 14.12.8 test with
  its tones fixed ($f_1 = 2f_0$, $f_2 = 3f_0 - \delta$; the standard tones
  8 kHz and 11.95 kHz are the defaults) and only the two in-band products at
  $f_0 \mp \delta$ counted:
  $d_\text{TDFD} = \sqrt{a_{f_2-f_1}^2 + a_{2f_1-f_2}^2} / (a_{f_1} + a_{f_2})$.

**Which one to run.** Modulation distortion for a wide-band device fed a
bass-heavy programme, difference-frequency for the top of the band where the
harmonics of the test tones fall outside it altogether, and DIM for anything
with feedback and a slew-rate limit. The reason to run any of them once a THD
is already in hand is that intermodulation products are *inharmonic*: the ear
reads them as roughness rather than as timbre and hears them at levels an equal
THD would pass unnoticed, and a device with memory — a thermally modulated
amplifier, a woofer Doppler-shifting a tweeter's output — can show a low THD
and poor modulation distortion.

One warning about the reference. IEC 60268-3 admits two presentations of the
same sidebands: against the output at $f_2$, which is the $d_\mathrm{m}$ this function
returns, and against the 4:1 combined reference voltage
$U_{2,\text{ref}} = 5\,U_{2,f_2}$, which gives $d'_\mathrm{m} = 5\,d_\mathrm{m}$. A figure lifted
from a datasheet may therefore be five times (14 dB) the one computed here, so
the reference has to be quoted with the number.

```python
from phonometry import electroacoustics

md = electroacoustics.modulation_distortion(signal, fs, f_low=60.0, f_high=7000.0)
print(md.d2, md.d3, md.smpte)                                       # 14.12.7
md.plot()   # carrier and modulation sidebands, d2/d3 annotated (needs matplotlib)
dfd2 = electroacoustics.difference_frequency_distortion(
    signal, fs, f1=13e3, f2=14e3, order=2
)
tdfd = electroacoustics.total_difference_frequency_distortion(signal, fs)         # 8/11.95 kHz
dim = electroacoustics.dynamic_intermodulation_distortion(signal, fs)             # DIM (15k/3.15k)
```

The `ModulationDistortionResult` is plottable: `.plot()` draws the output
amplitude at the carrier $f_2$ (the 0 dB reference) and the four modulation
sidebands at $f_2 \pm f_1$ and $f_2 \pm 2f_1$, the modulation counterpart of the
harmonic spectrum of section 1. The asymmetric ratio between the $n = 2$ and
$n = 3$ pairs reads the balance between the quadratic and cubic terms of the
non-linearity at a glance:

The other three tests return scalars, so the only way to see *which* products
each of them counts — the entire difference between them — is to mark them on
the spectrum of one device:

<ThemeImage src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jmrplens/phonometry/main/.github/images/intermodulation_tests.svg" alt="Three stacked spectra of the same weakly non-linear device: the difference-frequency test at 13 and 14 kHz with the second-order product at 1 kHz and the two third-order products at 12 and 15 kHz tagged; the total difference-frequency test at 8 and 11.95 kHz with only the two in-band products near 3.95 and 4.05 kHz shaded; and the dynamic-intermodulation signal, whose low-passed 3.15 kHz square-wave comb beats against the 15 kHz sine to leave a set of products below 15 kHz, each stemmed and labelled with its harmonic index k" width="92%" />

*Same device, three tests, three different sets of products. The
difference-frequency test counts the low product $f_2 - f_1$ and the two
third-order products that straddle the pair; the total difference-frequency
test keeps only the two products that the fixed tone pair drops near $f_0$; DIM
counts every $|k f_\text{square} \pm f_\text{sine}|$ that lands below the sine.
The denominator differs too — $a_{f_1} + a_{f_2}$ for the first two, the sine
amplitude alone for DIM, which is the quantity the
[errata registry](/phonometry/reference/errata/) entry is about.*

## 5. Frequency response and coherence (Bendat & Piersol)

Given an input `x` and the output `y` of a device, the **frequency response**
$H(f)$ is estimated from the Welch-averaged cross- and auto-spectra. Bendat &
Piersol (*Random Data*, 4th ed.) give two estimators, differing in which channel
carries the noise, plus the **ordinary coherence** $\gamma^2$, the fraction of
the output power linearly explained by the input:

$$
H_1 = \frac{G_{xy}}{G_{xx}}, \qquad H_2 = \frac{G_{yy}}{G_{yx}}, \qquad
\gamma^2 = \frac{|G_{xy}|^2}{G_{xx}\,G_{yy}} \in [0, 1].
$$

$H_1$ is unbiased when the noise is on the output, $H_2$ when it is on the
input; for a noiseless linear path both recover the true response and
$\gamma^2 = 1$. Additive output noise biases $H_2$ upward and pulls the
coherence down to $\mathrm{SNR}/(1+\mathrm{SNR})$.

<ThemeImage src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jmrplens/phonometry/main/.github/images/frequency_response.svg" alt="Three panels comparing the H1 and H2 estimators of the same band-pass device: with the noise on the output H1 tracks the true response and H2 rises above it towards the band edges, with the noise on the input the verdict inverts and H2 is the unbiased estimator, and the shared coherence panel below shows the measured gamma squared sitting on the dotted SNR over one plus SNR prediction" width="82%" />

*Pick the estimator whose noisy channel is the denominator: $H_1$ when the
noise is on the output, $H_2$ when it is on the input, and the same record
inverts the verdict when the noise moves. The coherence panel tells you how
much you are being lied to — its collapse at the band edges is not a property
of the device but of the signal-to-noise ratio there, and it is what says which
part of the magnitude curve to believe.*

```python
from phonometry import electroacoustics

res = electroacoustics.transfer_function(x, y, fs, estimator="H1")
print(res.magnitude_db, res.phase, res.coherence)
res.plot()   # Bode magnitude/phase + coherence (needs matplotlib)

freqs, gamma2 = electroacoustics.coherence(x, y, fs)
```

**Acquiring `x` and `y`.** Capture the reference and the response on two
sample-synchronous channels of *one* converter: two clocks drift against each
other and destroy the coherence at exactly the rate that makes the result look
like a noisy device. Tap the reference electrically after the generator rather
than reusing the ideal array you generated, so that whatever the generator
itself does appears in both channels and cancels. Excite with broadband noise
or a sweep at a level that keeps the device at the operating point of section
0. Remove the delay between the two channels — or make the Welch segment
several times longer than it — because an uncompensated delay both tilts the
$H_1$ phase and depresses $\gamma^2$ band by band. `transfer_function` picks
`nperseg` for about 4 Hz resolution when you do not give it one; the physical
arrangement is the one drawn on
[Two-channel system measurement](/phonometry/signals/spectra/system-measurement/).

**Reading the coherence.** A value below one has three physically distinct
causes and they are told apart by experiment: extraneous noise on either
channel (the coherence recovers when the excitation level is raised),
non-linearity in the device (it falls at high drive and recovers at low drive),
and bias errors — leakage where the resolution is too coarse near a sharp
resonance, and above all a delay longer than the segment. Averaging is what
converts the coherence into an error bar: the normalised random error of the
$|H_1|$ estimate is
$\varepsilon = \sqrt{1-\gamma^2}\,/\,(|\gamma|\sqrt{2 n_\mathrm{d}})$ for $n_\mathrm{d}$
averages, so $\gamma^2 = 0.9$ needs about 22 averages for a 5 % magnitude
error. As a working rule, a band whose coherence stays below roughly 0.9 is
**unmeasured** rather than measured badly, and its $H_1$ magnitude should not
be reported: low coherence is the measurement telling you that the output there
is not linearly explained by the input. A single segment gives
$\gamma^2 \equiv 1$ by construction and means nothing at all.

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal as sp
from phonometry import electroacoustics

fs = 48000
n = 400000
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
clean_x = rng.standard_normal(n)
b, a = sp.butter(2, [400.0, 4000.0], btype="band", fs=fs)   # device under test
clean_y = sp.lfilter(b, a, clean_x)

# Both columns: the same path, the noise moved from one channel to the other.
# 5 % on the output; 50 % on the input, because an input-noise bias is uniform
# across the band and a 5 % one would be invisible at this scale.
out_noise, in_noise = 0.05, 0.5
y_out = clean_y + rng.standard_normal(n) * np.sqrt(np.mean(clean_y ** 2)) * out_noise
x_in = clean_x + rng.standard_normal(n) * np.sqrt(np.mean(clean_x ** 2)) * in_noise
cases = (("Noise on the output - H1 is unbiased", clean_x, y_out),
         ("Noise on the input - H2 is unbiased", x_in, clean_y))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(11.4, 7.6), sharex=True,
                         gridspec_kw={"height_ratios": [2.0, 1.0]})
for col, (title, xx, yy) in enumerate(cases):
    h1 = electroacoustics.transfer_function(xx, yy, fs, estimator="H1")
    h2 = electroacoustics.transfer_function(xx, yy, fs, estimator="H2")
    _, h_true = sp.freqz(b, a, worN=h1.frequencies, fs=fs)
    pos = h1.frequencies > 0.0
    freqs = h1.frequencies[pos]

    ax_mag, ax_coh = axes[0][col], axes[1][col]
    ax_mag.semilogx(freqs, 20 * np.log10(np.abs(h_true[pos])), "--", label="True |H|")
    ax_mag.semilogx(freqs, h1.magnitude_db[pos], label="H1 = Gxy / Gxx")
    ax_mag.semilogx(freqs, h2.magnitude_db[pos], "-.", label="H2 = Gyy / Gyx")
    ax_mag.set(ylim=(-80.0, 12.0), title=title)
    ax_mag.legend(fontsize=8)

    # The coherence floor the section states, from the local SNR.
    if col == 0:   # output noise: the SNR follows |H|, so the floor collapses
        snr = (np.abs(h_true[pos]) ** 2 * np.mean(clean_x ** 2)
               / (np.mean(clean_y ** 2) * out_noise ** 2))
    else:          # input noise: the same ratio in every band
        snr = np.full_like(freqs, 1.0 / in_noise ** 2)
    ax_coh.semilogx(freqs, h1.coherence[pos], label="measured")
    ax_coh.semilogx(freqs, snr / (1.0 + snr), ":", label="SNR / (1 + SNR)")
    ax_coh.axhline(0.9, ls="--", lw=1.0)
    ax_coh.set(xlabel="Frequency [Hz]", ylim=(0.0, 1.05), xlim=(20.0, fs / 2))
    ax_coh.legend(fontsize=8)
axes[0][0].set_ylabel("Magnitude [dB]")
axes[1][0].set_ylabel("Coherence")
plt.show()
```
