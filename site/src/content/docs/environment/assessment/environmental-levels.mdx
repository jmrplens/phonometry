A community does not hear a single $L_\mathrm{Aeq}$: it hears a day whose evenings and
nights matter more, a source whose tones or impulses annoy beyond their
energy, and a measurement taken over a residual background with a finite
confidence. The tonal half of that sentence is this page; the impulsive half
belongs to
[Impulsive sound prominence](/phonometry/environment/assessment/impulsive-sound/),
which measures the adjustment ISO 1996-1 Table A.1 only tabulates. This page is the regulatory assessment chain built on top of
the measured period levels: the whole-day descriptors $L_\mathrm{den}$/$L_\mathrm{dn}$
and the composite rating levels of ISO 1996-1, and the ISO 1996-2
determination procedures that make the reported number defensible: the
tonal adjustment, the residual-noise correction and the measurement
uncertainty budget.

The level computation itself, the $L_\mathrm{eq}$/$L_\mathrm{Aeq}$ integrals,
the percentile levels $L_N$, SEL and the noise dose that produce the period
levels this page consumes, is
[Integrated and Statistical Levels](/phonometry/signals/levels/levels/); everything
here assumes those per-period values are already in hand.

## Environmental noise: Lden, Ldn and rating levels (ISO 1996-1)

Regulatory noise assessment weights evenings and nights more heavily.
`lden()` implements the day-evening-night level of ISO 1996-1:2016 (3.6.4:
+5 dB evening, +10 dB night, default 12/4/8 h periods, adjustable because
countries define them differently), `ldn()` the day-night variant (3.6.5),
and `composite_rating_level()` the general whole-day composite of clause 6.5
(Formulae 5-6) for arbitrary periods with source or character adjustments
(Table A.1: e.g. +5 dB regular impulsive, +12 dB highly impulsive, +3 to
+6 dB prominent tones):

```python
from phonometry import environment

l = environment.lden(63.2, 58.1, 51.4)                # from LAeq per period
r = environment.composite_rating_level([
    (63.2, 12, 0.0),                                  # day
    (58.1, 4, 5.0),                                   # evening (+5)
    (51.4, 8, 10.0),                                  # night  (+10)
])
print(round(l, 2), round(r, 2))                       # 62.66 62.66
```

The two agree to the last digit, which is the point of the snippet:
`lden` *is* `composite_rating_level` with the three ISO 1996-1 periods and their
time-of-day adjustments filled in.

<ThemeImage src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jmrplens/phonometry/main/.github/images/lden_profile.svg" alt="Synthetic 24-hour urban LAeq profile with day, evening and night bands, the +5 and +10 dB weighted period levels and the resulting Lden" width="80%" />

*The same day weighted three ways: the evening band is lifted 5 dB and the night
band 10 dB before the 12/4/8 h energy average, which is why an $L_\mathrm{den}$ of
64.3 dB sits above every hourly level of the night.*

**What 64.3 dB means.** The scale is worth carrying: a rural night sits in the
40s, a quiet residential street in the mid-50s, a façade on a busy urban arterial
in the high 60s, and the Environmental Noise Directive reports strategic maps in
5 dB $L_\mathrm{den}$ bands from 55 dB upward. The +5 and +10 dB weights are the price
of exposure at the hours when people are at home and asleep, so an evening or a
night source moves $L_\mathrm{den}$ far more than its energy share suggests: in the
profile above, the evening is 1.8 dB *quieter* than the day and lasts a third as
long, yet the +5 dB weight gives it 36 % of the energy that makes up the 64.3 dB
against the day's 51 %. Two limits on reading it. The reported indicator is a **long-term**
average: ISO 1996-1 defines its periods over a long-term time interval, normally
a year, so the worked day here is one sample of an $L_\mathrm{den}$ and not an
$L_\mathrm{den}$. And the number is an exposure, not a response; ISO 1996-1 carries
annexes that map an adjusted $L_\mathrm{den}$ or $L_\mathrm{dn}$ onto an estimated percentage
of a population highly annoyed, which is informative and is not implemented here.

### A year of city noise against its published period levels

The day above is synthetic, and it is a single day. The repository also keeps
a real year: the five-minute $L_\mathrm{Aeq}$ that two permanent monitors of
Dublin City Council logged through 2015, one at Ballymun and one at Ringsend.
The council's annual report for that year tabulates each site's annual day,
evening and night levels and its $L_\mathrm{den}$ in whole decibels (Fig. 3,
p. 11), and its text quotes the night levels, 58 dB(A) at Ballymun and
59 dB(A) at Ringsend (p. 11). The report averages logarithmically (p. 8) and
takes the evening from 19:00 to 23:00 and, as ISO 1996-1 does, the night from
23:00 to 07:00 (p. 9). The library takes period levels, not a series of
stamped intervals, so the snippet files each interval itself by the hour it
starts in (the stamps mark where an interval ends), takes the energy average
of each period and hands the three levels to `environment.lden`.

**The data.** Five-minute LAeq series from the Dublin City Council Ambient
Sound Monitoring Network (Ballymun and Ringsend, 2015), Smart Dublin /
[data.gov.ie](https://data.gov.ie/dataset/ambient-sound-monitoring-network),
licensed [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). Changes:
daily logs concatenated, sorted and de-duplicated; level values unmodified.

The file is read from a checkout of the repository, where the oracle test
reads it too; a wheel does not install the test data.

```python
import pathlib

import numpy as np
import phonometry
from phonometry import environment

# The committed series lives in the repository, so this runs from a checkout
#   (or an editable install); with your own monitor's export, load that instead.
repo = pathlib.Path(phonometry.__file__).resolve().parents[2]
# The council's annual report for 2015, Fig. 3 (p. 11): each site's annual Lday,
#   Levening, Lnight and Lden, in whole dB(A).
published = {"ballymun": (65, 63, 58, 67), "ringsend": (59, 60, 59, 65)}


def energy_mean(levels):
    """The energy average of a set of levels: the report's logarithmic mean."""
    return 10 * np.log10(np.mean(10 ** (levels / 10)))


with np.load(repo / "tests/data/audio/dublin/dcc_laeq_5min_2015.npz") as npz:
    for site, row in published.items():
        # Each stamp ends a five-minute interval, in seconds since 2015-01-01 00:00.
        stamps = int(npz[f"{site}_time0_s"]) + np.concatenate(([0], np.cumsum(npz[f"{site}_dt_s"])))
        laeq = npz[f"{site}_laeq_cdb"] / 100.0
        # File each interval by the hour it starts in: day 07-19, evening 19-23, night 23-07.
        hour = ((stamps - 300) // 3600) % 24
        night = (hour >= 23) | (hour < 7)
        evening = (hour >= 19) & (hour < 23)
        day = ~night & ~evening
        lday, levening, lnight = (energy_mean(laeq[period]) for period in (day, evening, night))
        levels = (lday, levening, lnight, environment.lden(lday, levening, lnight))
        # Lday, Levening, Lnight and Lden as computed, then as the report prints them.
        print(f"{laeq.size} intervals at {site}:", *(f"{level:.2f}" for level in levels),
              "dB(A), published", *row)
# 104242 intervals at ballymun: 64.57 63.34 57.98 66.69 dB(A), published 65 63 58 67
# 104657 intervals at ringsend: 59.35 59.66 58.81 65.38 dB(A), published 59 60 59 65
```

Both sites land on the report: every level rounds to the whole decibel Fig. 3
prints, the night levels of 57.98 dB(A) at Ballymun and 58.81 dB(A) at
Ringsend to the 58 and 59 its text quotes. The report prints whole decibels,
so
[the test](https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/blob/main/tests/environment/assessment/test_dublin_dcc_oracle.py)
that holds this computation allows half a decibel either way on all four
levels and asserts the rounding as well: rounded to the nearest decibel every
value matches, while truncated both night levels would fall a decibel short.
$L_\mathrm{den}$ shows the weighting at work: Ringsend is the louder site at
night, Ballymun the louder over the weighted whole day.

### `lden()` / `ldn()` / `composite_rating_level()` parameters

| Function | Key parameters | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `lden(lday, levening, lnight, period_hours=(12, 4, 8))` | period $L_\mathrm{Aeq}$ values [dB]; `period_hours` must sum to 24 | +5 dB evening, +10 dB night (3.6.4) |
| `ldn(lday, lnight, period_hours=(15, 9))` | | +10 dB night (3.6.5) |
| `composite_rating_level(periods)` | iterable of `(level, hours, adjustment)`; hours positive, finite and summing to 24 | General Formulae (5)-(6); adjustments per Table A.1 |

Where you put the microphone changes the number: ISO 1996-2 fixes the receiver
positions and their façade corrections. The diagram is measurement context;
apply the corrections to your levels **before** anything on this page:

<ThemeImage src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jmrplens/phonometry/main/.github/images/diagram_env_measurement.svg" alt="Environmental noise measurement positions per ISO 1996-2: free field, 2 m from the facade and flush-mounted, with their corrections" width="92%" />

| Microphone location | Correction to reach the incident (free-field) reference | The condition it holds under |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Incident sound field | 0 dB, this **is** the reference condition | No reflecting surface other than the ground close enough to matter |
| Flush-mounted on the façade | Subtract up to **6 dB**; exactly **5.7 dB** where the Annex B conditions are met | Façade plane within ±0.05 m over 1 m around the microphone, and more than 1 m from any edge of the wall |
| 0.5 m to 2 m in front of the façade | Subtract up to **3 dB**; exactly **3 dB** where the Annex B conditions are met | An otherwise open situation. **Not recommended at near-grazing incidence**, and a street canyon or a dense block can make the real difference much larger |

(ISO 1996-2:2017, 9.2.1 and Annex B. For general mapping in multi-storey
residential areas the same clause fixes the microphone height at
(4.0 ± 0.2) m.)

The reason the two corrections are not simply 6 and 3 dB is interference.
Flush-mounted, the direct and reflected waves are in phase everywhere on the
surface, so the doubling is coherent and broadband, which is why Annex B
recommends this position, not the 3 dB one, for stationary sources and low
frequencies. Two metres out, the path difference is finite and frequency
dependent, so the 3 dB is an ideal-case average that a canyon or a nearby
building breaks; Table B.1 puts the standard uncertainty of the correction at
0.4 dB for either position under traffic noise from all angles, but at **2.0 dB
for the 5.7 dB position and 1.0 dB for the 3 dB position under predominantly
grazing incidence**.

Two consequences for everything below. `lden`, `ldn` and
`composite_rating_level` consume the **corrected** level, so applying the
correction is the reader's step and no function on this page will do it. And the
reported level must always name its position and state whether a correction was
made: a rule the standard states in the same clause, and the reason an END
façade indicator, which is *defined* to include the reflection, must not be
corrected at all.

Combine with `laeq()` per time period to go from recordings to $L_\mathrm{den}$. The
tonal adjustment itself is justified by the tonal audibility route of the next
section (fed, for the ISO/PAS 20065 method, by
[Objective audibility of tones in noise](/phonometry/perception/psychoacoustics/tone-audibility/));
the `tone_to_noise_ratio()` / `prominence_ratio()` verdicts of
[Prominent Discrete Tones](/phonometry/perception/psychoacoustics/tone-prominence/) are
complementary emission screening, not the $K_\mathrm{t}$ basis.

## Determining levels: tonal adjustment, residual noise and uncertainty (ISO 1996-2)

ISO 1996-2:2017 is the **determination** part: how the measured level is turned
into a rating level and reported with its uncertainty. The rating-level *summation*
and the time-of-day penalties live in ISO 1996-1 (above); ISO 1996-2 supplies the
tonal adjustment, the residual-noise correction and the uncertainty budget.

### The measurement behind the numbers (Clause 5)

Everything on this page assumes a level that was obtained the way ISO 1996-2
requires, and the requirements are short enough to state in full.

The **whole chain** (microphones, cables, windscreens, recording devices and any
other accessory) meets class 1 of IEC 61672-1, for free-field or
random-incidence application as appropriate, and the filters meet class 1 of
IEC 61260 (5.1). A **windscreen is always used outdoors**, and it does not make
wind noise go away: the standard's own estimate for a 13 mm microphone in a
90 mm windscreen is an A-weighted wind-noise level of about
$-18 + 70\log_{10} v$ dB with the wind normal to the diaphragm and
$-32 + 83\log_{10} v$ dB with it parallel, $v$ in m/s, which is 31 dB(A) at
5 m/s and 52 dB(A) at 10 m/s, i.e. above the residual of a quiet rural night
before the wind is anywhere near a gale. Class 1 tolerance limits themselves
only apply between −10 °C and +50 °C.

**Calibration** (5.2) is a check at the start *and* the end of every measurement
with a class 1 IEC 60942 sound calibrator. The acceptance criterion is explicit:
without any readjustment the two consecutive checks must differ by **≤ 0.5 dB**,
and if they do not, *everything measured since the previous satisfactory check is
discarded*. That is the single rule that most often costs a campaign a day, and
it is why the closing check is not optional. For monitoring over several days or
more, ISO 20906 takes over.

**Verification** (5.3) is a valid certificate of compliance against
IEC 61672-3, IEC 61260 and IEC 60942, issued by a laboratory meeting ISO/IEC
17025 with traceability to national standards; the recommended interval is once
a year and the **maximum allowable interval is two years**.

The **meteorological** sensors carry their own maximum permissible errors (5.4),
and they are what the uncertainty budget below silently assumes: ±0.5 K for
temperature, ±5.0 % for relative humidity, ±0.5 hPa for barometric pressure,
±0.5 m/s for wind speed and ±5° for wind direction.

Finally, a measured environmental level is only reportable with its context.
Clause 13 lists what has to be recorded: the time, day and place; the
instrumentation and how it was calibrated; the measured **and corrected** levels;
the percentile levels with the base they were computed on; the estimated expanded
uncertainty **with its coverage probability**; the residual sound levels during
the measurement; the time intervals; a description of the site including ground
cover and the heights of microphone and source above it; and the source operating
conditions, including how many events of each category were captured.

### The weather and the time to measure (Clauses 6, 8 and 9)

The instrument is the smaller half of the requirement. The other half is the
weather the level is taken in, for how long and how many times, and ISO 1996-2
states each of them.

**What is recorded about the weather** (8.1, 9.3.5). For long-term and
short-term measurements alike the meteorological parameters are measured: as a
minimum wind speed, wind direction, relative humidity and temperature, with
information about atmospheric stability, measured directly or taken from cloud
cover and time of day, and about precipitation if there is any. Wind speed and
wind direction are measured at a height of 10 m. A lower mast is admitted for
practical reasons, but it raises the uncertainty, because the data and the
experience behind the standard were gathered at 10 m.

**When the weather matters** (8.1). Over soft ground the level varies only
modestly with the weather as long as $(h_\mathrm{s} + h_\mathrm{r})/D \ge 0.1$
(Formula (11)): source height plus receiver height against the horizontal
distance between them. A microphone at 4 m and a source at 0.5 m satisfy it out
to 45 m; over hard ground larger distances may be acceptable. Beyond that
distance the propagation is classed by how far the sound paths bend in the
direction of the shortest distance from the receiver to the source, through the
ratio of the distance $D$ to the radius of curvature $R_\mathrm{cur}$ of the
paths, which is negative when they curve upwards. Table 4 gives four
**meteorological windows**:

| Window | $D/R_\mathrm{cur}$ | Representative value | Propagation | Typical wind component at 10 m |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| M1 | below −0.04 | −0.08 | unfavourable | below 1 m/s by day, below −1 m/s by night |
| M2 | −0.04 to 0.04 | 0.00 | neutral | 1 m/s to 3 m/s |
| M3 | 0.04 to 0.12 | 0.08 | favourable | 3 m/s to 6 m/s |
| M4 | above 0.12 | 0.16 | very favourable | above 6 m/s by day, −1 m/s or more by night |

The wind component is the one along the line from the source to the receiver,
and a minus sign means that the wind blows from the receiver towards the
source. The standard calls the table simplified and admits any other
description that achieves the same curvatures; $R_\mathrm{cur}$ can also be
calculated from the measured meteorological parameters with the informative
Annex A, and the description may not suit very high sources such as wind
turbines. Table 4 gives no angular sector for the wind: the windows are defined
by $D/R_\mathrm{cur}$, and the wind components are the typical values that go
with them.

**One measurement or a few** (6.1, 8.2). For a single measurement to mean
anything, the minimum is that it is taken under favourable propagation and that
the source operation is monitored while it lasts (6.1). Clause 8.2 puts it as a
recommendation for one or a few short-term measurements: take them in window M3
or M4, or where Formula (11) holds. Then, unless better information is
available, the standard uncertainty of the meteorology is 2 dB for
$D \le 400$ m (Formula (12)), which is the 2.00 dB row of the budget
[further down](#measurement-uncertainty-clause-4-annex-f), and
$(1 + D/400\ \mathrm{m})$ dB beyond that distance (Formula (13)): 3 dB at
800 m. The clause warns that it may not suit aircraft noise entirely, because
the direction of take-off and landing follows the wind along the runway, and
that some microphone locations never have favourable propagation.

**Rain** (8.3). Results taken with precipitation on the windscreen are
discarded unless its effect can be shown to have been negligible. A windscreen
that has been rained on stays altered after the rain stops: for a standard 9 cm
windscreen the effect can be significant above 1 kHz once more than 1 mm has
fallen, and it lasts until the windscreen has dried, a time that Formula (14)
estimates from the amount of rain, as an example only. Data taken meanwhile
remain usable if the added uncertainty is taken into account.

**How long** (9.1.2, 9.3.2.2). The measurement time interval covers all the
significant variations of the emission. If the noise is periodic the interval
preferably spans a whole number of several periods, or else several intervals
each represent a part of the cycle and together the whole of it. For single
events, such as an aircraft fly-over, the intervals are chosen so that the
sound exposure level $L_{E,T}$ of the event can be determined. Against the
weather there is a number: $L_{\mathrm{eq},T}$ is measured in frequency bands
for at least 30 min, to average the meteorological variations along the path,
unless Formula (11) holds or the propagation is favourable, when 10 min is
usually sufficient (9.3.2.2), and 10 min is also the minimum averaging time of
a short-term measurement that needs favourable conditions over a distance
Formula (11) does not cover (9.1.2). Both minima are increased as far as a
representative sample of the source operation requires (Clause 7).

**How many** (6.1, 6.2, 9.1.1). The standard fixes no number of intervals and
no upper limit of uncertainty: it leaves the accuracy to aim for to whoever
uses the result, and requires the uncertainty to be determined. It offers two
strategies: a single measurement under very well-defined meteorological
conditions with the source operation carefully monitored, or a long-term
measurement, or many sampled ones, spread over time with the meteorology
monitored. In the second, each result is filed in a window, a combination of an
emission condition (day, evening or night, for instance) and one of the four
meteorological windows. The level of a window is the energy mean of the
independent measurements made in it (Formula (6)), and the long-term level
weights the windows by how often each occurs (Formula (5)), so a long-term
campaign includes as many of the important emission and propagation conditions
as it can, and above all the windows that contribute most (9.1.1). Two
measurements count as independent only when enough time separates them. Table 3
gives the guidance: for road traffic 24 h under 100 m, 48 h from 100 m to 300 m
and 72 h beyond, by day and by night. The table has rows for rail, industry and
aircraft too, and in several of their cells it is the operation of the source
that sets the minimum.

### Tonal adjustment (engineering method, ISO 1996-2:2007 Annex C)

The 2017
edition dropped the self-contained algorithm and refers to ISO/PAS 20065
instead ([Objective audibility of tones in noise](/phonometry/perception/psychoacoustics/tone-audibility/));
what it keeps is the Annex J table that converts a *mean* audibility into an
integer adjustment. The 2007 algorithm is the one implemented here, because it
runs on a single spectrum. From the energy-summed tone
level $L_{p\mathrm{t}}$ and the masking-noise level $L_{p\mathrm{n}}$ in the critical band around a
tone, the audibility above the masking threshold is
$\Delta L_\mathrm{ta} = L_{p\mathrm{t}} - L_{p\mathrm{n}} + 2 + \log_{10}[1 + (f_\mathrm{c}/502)^{2.5}]$ dB (Formula (C.3)),
and the adjustment is $K_\mathrm{t} = 0$ for $\Delta L_\mathrm{ta} < 4$, $K_\mathrm{t} = \Delta L_\mathrm{ta} - 4$
for $4 \le \Delta L_\mathrm{ta} \le 10$ and $K_\mathrm{t} = 6$ above (Formulae (C.4)–(C.6)). The
critical bandwidth is 100 Hz up to 500 Hz and 20 % of $f_\mathrm{c}$ above (Table C.1).
The one-third-octave **survey method** (`assess_tonal_survey`) flags a band
exceeding both neighbours by 15/8/5 dB (low/mid/high), and
`tonal_adjustment_from_mean_audibility` maps the ISO/PAS 20065 mean audibility to
$K_\mathrm{t}$ (Table J.1).

The chain is three functions, not one: `tonal_audibility(Lpt, Lpn, fc)` returns
$\Delta L_\mathrm{ta}$ from Formula (C.3), `tonal_adjustment(dLta)` maps it to $K_\mathrm{t}$
through Formulae (C.4)-(C.6), and `assess_tonal_audibility(...)` combines both
with `critical_bandwidth` into the plottable result the snippets below use.
The two lower-level functions are the ones the
[Spanish regulation guide](/phonometry/environment/assessment/spanish-noise-regulation/)
and the [wind-turbine guide](/phonometry/environment/sources/wind-turbine-noise/)
refer to by name, so they are worth calling directly when only one step is
wanted.

<ThemeImage src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jmrplens/phonometry/main/.github/images/tonal_audibility.svg" alt="ISO 1996-2 tonal adjustment Kt as a piecewise function of the tonal audibility: zero below 4 dB, rising linearly to 6 dB between 4 and 10 dB, and 6 dB above, with the four Annex C.5 worked examples and a mid-range tone marked" width="80%" />

*The adjustment saturates at both ends: below 4 dB of audibility a tone costs
nothing and above 10 dB it costs no more than 6 dB, so the whole decision lives
in a 6 dB window of $\Delta L_\mathrm{ta}$. All four Annex C.5 examples land in or above
that window.*

### Residual-noise correction (Clause 10.4)

`residual_sound_correction()`
applies $L = 10\log_{10}(10^{L'/10} - 10^{L_\text{res}/10})$ (Formula (16)). With a
residual within 3 dB of the measured level no correction is allowed: the
*uncorrected* measured level $L'$ is then the reportable value, as an upper
bound of the specific sound (exposed as `reportable_upper_bound`, with
`reliable=False`). `gaussian_residual_level()` estimates the residual from
percentile levels (Annex I) and rejects inverted percentile orderings.

### Measurement uncertainty (Clause 4, Annex F)

`metrology.combined_standard_uncertainty()`, the one root-sum-square the
library has, forms $u = \sqrt{\sum (c_j u_j)^2}$ (Formula (2)) and
`environmental_expanded_uncertainty()` applies $k = 2$ (95 %) or $k = 1.3$ (80 %);
`residual_correction_uncertainty()` carries the residual-correction sensitivity
(Formulae (F.7)/(F.8)) and `uncertainty_from_repeated_measurements()` the
repeated-measurement standard uncertainty: the primary energy-domain route
(Formulae (17)+(19)), with the level-domain Note 2 substitute (Formula (20))
reported alongside as `approximate_uncertainty` and a warning when the levels
spread beyond 3 dB, where the substitute grossly inflates.

```python
from phonometry import environment, metrology

tonal = environment.assess_tonal_audibility(54.1, 45.2, 430.0)  # TonalAssessmentResult
kt = tonal.adjustment                                             # 6 dB
tonal.plot()   # this audibility on the Kt curve, as in the figure above
corr = environment.residual_sound_correction(measured_level=58.0, residual_level=50.0)
print(round(corr.corrected_level, 2), corr.reliable)          # 57.25 True

# Table G.2: one hour beside a road under favourable conditions. The five
# contributions are the measured level (0.5 dB scaled by the F.7 sensitivity),
# the source operating conditions (1000 vehicles counted, 10/sqrt(1000)),
# the meteorology, the microphone-location correction and the residual.
u = metrology.combined_standard_uncertainty([0.59, 0.3, 2.0, 0.40, 0.38])
print(round(u, 2))                                             # 2.18 dB (G.2)
print(round(environment.environmental_expanded_uncertainty(u), 2))           # 4.36 dB (k = 2)
```

The list is not five anonymous numbers. It is the Table G.2 budget of
ISO 1996-2:2017 and every entry has a name, a source and a size:

| Contribution | Where it comes from | $c_j u_j$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Measured level $L_\mathrm{eq,1h}$ | $u = 0.5$ dB (which already absorbs the class 1 sound level meter's own 0.5 dB default), multiplied by the residual-correction sensitivity $c_{L'}$ of Formula (F.7) | 0.59 dB |
| Source operating conditions | 1 000 vehicles counted, $10/\sqrt{1000}$ (Formula (8)) | 0.30 dB |
| **Meteorology** | favourable conditions, Formula (12) | **2.00 dB** |
| Microphone location | the +5.7 dB flush-mounted correction of Annex B | 0.40 dB |
| Residual sound | $L_\mathrm{res} = 50$ dB with $u = 2$ dB, through the sensitivity $c_\mathrm{res}$ of Formula (F.8) | 0.38 dB |

`metrology.combined_standard_uncertainty` also accepts $(u_j, c_j)$ **pairs**, so the
sensitivity coefficients need not be pre-multiplied by hand, and
`residual_correction_uncertainty` supplies exactly the two coefficients of
Formulae (F.7)/(F.8), both of which grow without bound as the residual
approaches the measured level, which is the same 3 dB cliff as above seen from
the uncertainty side.

Now read the column. The meteorology term alone is 2.0 dB and it sets the
answer: drop it and the combined uncertainty falls from 2.18 dB to 0.86 dB. The
instrument is nowhere near the top of the list. **A class 1 meter does not make
an environmental measurement accurate.** How representative the propagation
conditions and the source operation were during that hour is what decides it, and
the cheapest way to shrink $u$ is therefore to measure more often, which is what
`uncertainty_from_repeated_measurements` is for. What the standard asks of the
weather, and for how long, is in
[the weather and the time to measure](#the-weather-and-the-time-to-measure-clauses-6-8-and-9)
above.

## Quick answers

### What penalties does Lden apply to evening and night noise?

$L_\mathrm{den}$, the day-evening-night level of ISO 1996-1:2016 (3.6.4), adds
+5 dB to the evening level and +10 dB to the night level before
energy-averaging the whole day, with default periods of 12, 4 and 8 hours,
adjustable because countries define them differently. The day-night variant
$L_\mathrm{dn}$ (3.6.5) keeps only the +10 dB night penalty.
