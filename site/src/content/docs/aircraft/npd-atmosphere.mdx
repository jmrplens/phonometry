The noise-power-distance (NPD) curves of the ANP database were measured in
whatever air the certification tests happened to have, and then normalised to
one atmosphere so that curves from different aircraft can be compared. That
atmosphere is **SAE AIR-1845**: the arithmetic mean of the attenuation rates
recorded in European and US certification tests. It is not a temperature and a
humidity; no real air absorbs exactly like it in every band, and ECAC Doc 29
calls it a purely notional atmosphere.

An airport is not notional. A cold, humid coastal field absorbs less at high
frequency than the average test day, so its aircraft are louder far from the
runway than the database says; a hot, dry one can absorb more. ECAC Doc 29
Vol. 2 Appendix D recalculates the NPD curves for the air of a study, and this
guide runs it: from the AIR-1845 rates, through the spectral class that each
NPD carries, to revised curves that feed the event levels and contours of
[Airport Noise (ECAC Doc 29)](/phonometry/aircraft/airport-noise/) and
[The ANP fleet database](/phonometry/aircraft/anp-fleet/).

## 1. The AIR-1845 atmosphere

Table D-1 is the whole of it: one attenuation rate per one-third-octave band,
in decibels per 100 m, from 50 Hz to 10 kHz.

```python
from phonometry import aircraft

rates = aircraft.SAE_AIR1845_ATTENUATION_DB_PER_100M
print(rates[1000.0], rates[10000.0])   # 0.59 9.836 dB/100 m
```

The table is read-only, like every table the library publishes. Appendix D
suggests computing the rates of the study atmosphere and comparing them with
these before deciding whether an adjustment is needed at all; the increment of
section 3 is that comparison carried through to the level.

## 2. The spectral class of an NPD

An NPD table gives A-weighted levels, and absorption acts band by band, so the
recalculation needs a spectrum. Every ANP aircraft is assigned two **spectral
classes**, one for approach and one for departure: the average unweighted
spectrum of a family of aircraft at the time of the maximum level, at 1000 ft,
normalised to the same AIR-1845 rates and, for historical reasons, to 70 dB in
the 1 kHz band (Doc 29 Vol. 2, G4.3). The database ships 41 of them in
`Spectral_classes.csv`, and phonometry now reads it.

```python
from phonometry import aircraft

db = aircraft.load_anp_database()
ac = db.aircraft("747100")
print(ac.departure_spectral_class_id, ac.approach_spectral_class_id)   # 107 209
spectrum = ac.spectral_class("D")
print(spectrum.description, spectrum.levels_db[13])   # 4-Engine.Tfan 70.0 dB at 1 kHz
print(db.spectral_class(103).description)             # 2-Engine.HighByPass.Tfan
```

## 3. The increment, in three steps

With the spectral class $L_{n,\mathrm{ref}}(d_\mathrm{ref})$ at
$d_\mathrm{ref}$ = 1000 ft = 304.8 m and the AIR-1845 rate
$\alpha_{n,\mathrm{ref}}$ of band $n$, Appendix D first adds the reference
attenuation back (Eq. D-1), then takes the spectrum to each NPD distance $d_i$
twice, once in each atmosphere (Eqs. D-2 and D-3), and finally A-weights and
sums both (Eq. D-4). The rates are the decibels lost over 100 m of path, as
Table D-1 prints them, and the distances are in metres, so every attenuation
term carries the conversion $d/(100\,\mathrm{m})$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_n(d_\mathrm{ref}) &= L_{n,\mathrm{ref}}(d_\mathrm{ref}) + \alpha_{n,\mathrm{ref}}\,\frac{d_\mathrm{ref}}{100\,\mathrm{m}} \\
L_{n,\mathrm{ref}}(d_i) &= L_n(d_\mathrm{ref}) - 20\lg(d_i/d_\mathrm{ref}) - \alpha_{n,\mathrm{ref}}\,\frac{d_i}{100\,\mathrm{m}} \\
L_{n,\mathrm{atm}}(d_i) &= L_n(d_\mathrm{ref}) - 20\lg(d_i/d_\mathrm{ref}) - \delta_n(d_i)\,\frac{d_i}{100\,\mathrm{m}} \\
\Delta L(d_i) &= 10\lg\sum_n 10^{(L_{n,\mathrm{atm}}(d_i) - A_n)/10} - 10\lg\sum_n 10^{(L_{n,\mathrm{ref}}(d_i) - A_n)/10}
\end{aligned}
$$

Doc 29 prints the same equations with the rates in dB/m. At 1 kHz the rate of
0.59 dB/100 m puts back $0.59 \times 304.8/100$ = 1.80 dB over $d_\mathrm{ref}$,
the 1.798 dB of Table D-3a, and the 70.0 dB of the class becomes the 71.8 dB
that Table D-2 prints at the source. $\delta_n(d_i)$ is the rate of the
specified air in the same unit, which under ARP 5534 also depends on the path
length and the pressure, so $\delta_n(d_i)\,d_i/(100\,\mathrm{m})$ is its band
attenuation over the path. $A_n$ is the A-weighting as an attenuation: the
nominal values of IEC 61672-1, to 0.1 dB, which Doc 29 names without printing
and which are the ones that reproduce its tables. The increment $\Delta L$ is
added to the NPD level at the same distance, for every power setting and for
$L_\mathrm{max}$ and $L_E$ alike: the change of air is taken to alter the
spectrum, not the shape of the level time history. Because $\Delta L$ is a
difference of two sums over the same spectrum, the 70 dB normalisation of the
class drops out of it.

```python
import numpy as np
from phonometry import aircraft

db = aircraft.load_anp_database()
increment = aircraft.npd_atmosphere_increment(
    db.spectral_class(103), temperature_c=10.0, relative_humidity_percent=80.0
)
print(round(increment.reference_attenuation_db[13, 3], 3))   # 1.798 dB, 1 kHz over 1000 ft
print(round(increment.source_spectrum_db[13], 1))            # 71.8 dB, Eq. D-1
print(np.round(increment.reference_levels_dba, 1))   # 97.0 90.3 ... 35.3 dBA
print(np.round(increment.increment_db, 1))
# [0.1 0.3 0.4 0.7 1.2 1.8 2.1 2.4 2.9 3.6] dB, 200 ft to 25000 ft
```

Those are the numbers of Table D-4. At 10 °C and 80 % the air absorbs less than
the AIR-1845 average in the bands that matter, so the NPD levels rise, by a
tenth of a decibel at 200 ft and by 3.6 dB at 25000 ft. The temperature and the
humidity are keyword-only and have no default: the AIR-1845 atmosphere is not a
pair of them, so there is no reference value to fall back to.

## 4. Two absorption routes

Appendix D offers two ways to compute $\delta_n(d_i)$, and `absorption=` picks
one.

**SAE ARP 5534** (`"arp5534"`, the default and the route Doc 29 recommends)
evaluates the pure-tone coefficient at the exact mid-band frequency and maps the
pure-tone attenuation over the path to a band attenuation with the SAE Method,
so the band attenuation depends on the path length and on the pressure. It is
[`sae_band_attenuation`](/phonometry/aircraft/aircraft-noise/) of the
certification guide. Its pure-tone coefficient is the one of ISO 9613-1 with the
saturation vapour pressure that ARP 5534 prints in its Eqs. 5-6, the longer form
of ANSI S1.26; with the shorter ISO 9613-1 form 57 of the 240 cells of Table
D-3c come out up to 0.036 dB off, and with Eqs. 5-6 all 240 agree to the last
printed digit.

**SAE ARP 866A** (`"arp866a"`, kept by Doc 29 for transition) is an attenuation
rate per band that depends on temperature and humidity only. SAE ARP 866A is
not reachable as a document of its own; its formula is given in ISO 3891:1978
Annex A, which is where phonometry implements it from, as
`arp866a_attenuation`:

```python
import numpy as np
from phonometry import aircraft

absorption = aircraft.arp866a_attenuation(
    [1000.0, 10000.0], temperature_c=10.0, relative_humidity_percent=80.0
)
print(absorption.evaluation_frequencies_hz)             # [1000. 9000.] Hz
print(np.round(absorption.coefficient_db_per_100m, 3))  # [0.439 9.774] dB/100 m

for reading in ("quadratic", "linear"):
    band = aircraft.arp866a_attenuation(
        [4000.0], temperature_c=10.0, relative_humidity_percent=80.0,
        eta_interpolation=reading,
    )
    print(reading, np.round(band.path_attenuation_db(7620.0), 1))
# quadratic [203.1], linear [204.4] dB over 25000 ft; Table D-3b prints 204.355
```

Two details of Annex A decide those numbers. Above 4 kHz the formula is
evaluated at the lower edge of the band, not at its centre, because the steep
fall of an aircraft spectrum puts most of a high band's energy at its lower end;
Table 2 prints those frequencies rounded, 4500, 5600, 7100 and 9000 Hz, and the
10 kHz band above is evaluated at 9 kHz. And the factor $\eta(\delta)$ comes
from Table 1, whose note says "a form of quadratic interpolation shall be used"
without saying which, and the two documents that print this attenuation read
it differently. ISO 3891's own Table 10 (80 %, -10 °C to 40 °C) is reproduced
in all its 264 cells by the parabola through three neighbouring entries of
Table 1, which is the default of `arp866a_attenuation`; linear interpolation
misses 11 of them. Doc 29 Table D-3b, and through it Tables D-5 and D-6c, is
reproduced by linear interpolation, which the quadratic misses by up to 1.2 dB
at 25000 ft, so the Appendix D route reads $\eta$ linearly. The two part only
where $\delta$ falls in the curved part of Table 1, below 6.50 (from there on
Table 1 prints 0.200 and both readings hold it), and the increments they give
differ by a few thousandths of a decibel.

*The attenuation of Tables D-3a to D-3c at the longest NPD distance. Where a
curve lies below the dashed AIR-1845 one the specified air absorbs less than the

absorbs less than ARP 866A and both less than AIR-1845.*

## 5. Revised curves, and the chain that reads them

`revise_npd_curves` adds the increment to every power setting of an
`AnpNpdCurves`, and `AnpDatabase.revised_npd_curves` does it with the aircraft's
own spectral class. The revised curves are an ordinary `AnpNpdCurves`, so
`level`, `plot` and the Doc 29 functions read them like the database's own.

```python
from phonometry import aircraft

db = aircraft.load_anp_database()
revised = db.revised_npd_curves(
    "747100", "D", "SEL", temperature_c=30.0, relative_humidity_percent=30.0
)
before = float(revised.original.levels[0, -1])
after = float(revised.revised.levels[0, -1])
print(round(before, 1), round(after, 1))   # 63.4 62.9 dB at 25000 ft, 20000 lb
revised.plot()
```

*A hot, dry day on a four-engined departure. The increment is negative: this
air absorbs more than the AIR-1845 average in the bands that carry the
A-weighted level at long range, so beyond about 1 km the revised curves fall

For a whole event or a contour there is no need to revise anything by hand. The
event and contour wiring of the database takes the humidity, recalculates both
the SEL and the LAmax tables with the aircraft's spectral class, and uses the
same temperature and pressure it already reads for the impedance adjustment:
there is one air at the field, and the humidity completes it.

```python
from phonometry import aircraft

db = aircraft.load_anp_database()
receiver = [6000.0, 0.0, 0.0]
reference = db.event_level("747100", receiver, "D", temperature_c=10.0)
humid = db.event_level(
    "747100", receiver, "D", temperature_c=10.0, relative_humidity_percent=80.0
)
print(round(reference.level, 1), round(humid.level, 1))   # 97.8 99.1 dB SEL
```

Leave `relative_humidity_percent` unset and nothing changes: the curves stay in
the AIR-1845 atmosphere, as they always have. With an `Aerodrome`, the
temperature and the field pressure come from it.

## 6. Checked against the worked example

Appendix D closes with a worked example that prints every intermediate table,
and each one is a row of the conformance report: the source spectra of Table
D-2, the three attenuation tables D-3a to D-3c, the A-weighted levels and
increments of Tables D-4 and D-5, and the revised JETW data of Tables D-6b and
D-6c, cell by cell at the precision they are printed to. All of them agree to
the last digit but two. In Table D-3b, 32 of the 240 cells, the longest paths at
the highest frequencies, are printed about 6 parts per million below the ISO
3891 formula, at most 0.0045 dB of 745 dB; the row allows for it and says so.
And the last row of Table D-6c is printed as a copy of the last row of Table
D-6b, which is in the [errata register](/phonometry/reference/errata/) together
with a sentence of the appendix that pairs Tables D-3b and D-3c with each
other's method.

ISO 3891 is checked on its own terms as well: its Table 10 is a row of the
report, all 264 cells from -10 °C to 40 °C to the printed digit. Its Table 9,
at 70 %, is not, and the formula misses 15 of its cells by one printed unit. In
seven of them the humidity drops out of the formula, so the two tables have to
print the same value, and Table 9 prints one unit more than Table 10; those
seven are in the errata register too. The other eight are cells where the
humidity does enter, and each lies within 0.014 dB/100 m of a rounding
boundary: no reading of Table 1 tried (the three-point quadratic, linear,
monotone cubic, Akima, cubic spline) reproduces them all, and no second table
contradicts them, so they are left as they are printed and not registered as
misprints.
