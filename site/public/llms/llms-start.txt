# Start

phonometry computes acoustic quantities from the text of the standards that
define them: ISO, IEC, ANSI and ASTM, the CNOSSOS-EU annex to Directive
2002/49/EC, and the ICAO and ECAC aircraft documents. Every metric names the
clause it implements. What that buys you, and how it is checked, is
[Why phonometry](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/why-phonometry/).

Seven short pages, meant to be read once before anything else. Each answers one
question, and they are in the order the questions arrive.

**Can I install it and get a number out?**
[Getting Started](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/getting-started/) installs the library,
runs a first one-third-octave analysis on a synthetic signal, then anchors that
analysis to a calibrator tone so the levels are decibels re 20 µPa rather than
decibels re nothing, reduces them to one A-weighted level, and states what a
recording must satisfy for any of it to hold. It stops one stage short of a
meter: the Fast and Slow ballistics, $L_{\mathrm{A}E}$, $L_\mathrm{Cpeak}$ and the percentile
levels are in [Build a sound level
meter](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/sound-level-meter/), which runs the whole chain end
to end on one page, and [Calibration and
dBFS](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/metrology/calibration/) is the deep guide behind the
one step that matters most. [Environments](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/environments/)
says how the same install goes with uv or conda, in a notebook or a browser,
in a container and under a type checker.

**My code is written against 3.3 and the imports fail.**
[Upgrading from 3.3 to 4.0](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/upgrading/) is the map: the one rule
that moves almost every import, a resolver to paste when you would rather not
look a name up, the four places where the obvious fix is the wrong one, and
the seventy-eight names that are gone.

**I have a job, not a subject. Which page is it?**
[What do you need to measure?](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/tasks/) indexes the library by
the task instead of the topic: measure a reverberation time, check a wall
against a building code, rate a machine's sound power, decide whether a worker
is over the exposure limit.

**Where is the thing I came for?**
[All guides](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/) is the map: every guide in the library,
grouped by the topic it belongs to, with a line on each.

**Should I trust the number?**
[Why phonometry](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/why-phonometry/) sets out what the library
is for and how it is validated against the standards it implements, with the
tone-burst check worked through against the acceptance limits.

**Who is answerable for it, and how do I cite it?**
[About](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/about/) states who maintains it, how to cite it and
under what licence.

[Support the project](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/support/) lists the ways to help,
starting with the primary sources the library still lacks.

## Two things to settle before any guide works

**Which reference frame a level is in.** A level is either *physical*, in
dB SPL, anchored by a recorded calibrator tone or by a known microphone
sensitivity, or *digital*, in dBFS relative to full scale. The two are not
interchangeable, and most guides assume the first: a level function handed raw
soundcard samples returns a number whose reference is arbitrary, which looks
exactly like a valid answer. [Calibration and
dBFS](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/metrology/calibration/) settles it.

**That almost everything downstream consumes bands.** Below the raw signal
there is one decomposition: fractional-octave bands whose −3 dB edges sit on
the ANSI S1.11 / IEC 61260-1 nominal frequencies. A loudness model, a room
parameter and an environmental rating all start from it, which is why the
band-filtering page is the one prerequisite that turns up everywhere: [Filter
Banks](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/filters/filter-banks/).

## What a guide looks like

Worth knowing before opening one, because it is what lets you decide in thirty
seconds whether a page answers your question. Every guide opens with the
standard it implements, the quantities that standard defines and the
assumptions the implementation makes; then comes runnable code and the figure
it draws; and it closes with a "What this guide covers" block that states
plainly which clauses, annexes and methods are implemented and which are not.
The last of those is the part a reviewer asks about, and it is deliberately the
part written most bluntly.

## If you already know what you need

- A first measurement carried end to end: [Build a sound level meter](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/sound-level-meter/).
- The whole inventory, by topic: [All guides](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/).
- A symbol you have but cannot name: the [glossary](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/glossary/), with its unit, its defining clause and the guide that computes it.
- Evidence that a number is defensible: the [conformance report](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/conformance/), which prints each standard's own expected value beside the computed one.
- A printed expected value that disagrees with the library: the [errata registry](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/errata/), which says which of the two is wrong and why.

The assumed starting point is Python 3.12 or newer with working NumPy and
SciPy, and enough acoustics to know what a one-third-octave band and a sound
pressure level are.

## What Start is not

This is not a tutorial series, and it is not the API. Function signatures and
argument types are in the generated API reference. The derivations, the
numerical conformance report, the errata register for defects found in the
published standards themselves, the glossary of symbols and the bibliography
are all in [Reference](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/). And the acoustics itself is
assumed rather than taught: the guides explain the method a standard
prescribes and why it is written that way, not what a decibel is.

# Getting Started

## Installation

The commands below are pip's. [Environments](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/environments/) takes uv,
conda, Jupyter, JupyterLite, Docker and type checkers in turn.

**Option 1: From PyPI (Recommended)**

```bash
pip install phonometry
```

phonometry needs Python 3.12 or newer, with NumPy 2.0.2 and SciPy 1.14.0
or later. Each of those floors, and each floor of the extras, is the oldest
release the library's tests pass with, and a CI job installs exactly those
releases on Python 3.12 to keep it so: matplotlib 3.10.7 for `[plot]`,
reportlab 4.3.0 and svglib 0.9.1 (but not 1.4.0) for `[report]`, numba
0.60.0 for `[perf]`, python-soundfile 0.12.0 for `[audio]` and torch 2.3.0
with array-api-compat 1.5.1 for `[torch]`. Python 3.13 and 3.14 have floors
of their own for NumPy, SciPy, numba and torch, whose oldest releases have no
wheel for them: the oldest releases that do, which the same job installs on
each, on Python 3.13 NumPy 2.1.0, SciPy 1.14.1, numba 0.61.0 and torch 2.5.0,
and on Python 3.14 NumPy 2.3.2, SciPy 1.16.1, numba 0.63.0 and torch 2.9.0.
The Python 3.13 wheel of torch 2.5.0 is for Linux on x86-64 only, where the
job runs; on macOS on ARM, Windows on x86-64 and Linux on ARM the first is
torch 2.6.0, the oldest that installs there.

Optional extras:

```bash
pip install "phonometry[plot]"    # matplotlib, for filter response plots and result .plot() methods
pip install "phonometry[perf]"    # numba, faster 'impulse' time weighting
pip install "phonometry[report]"  # reportlab + svglib, so result .report() methods render normative PDF fiches (their figure panel needs [plot] too)
pip install "phonometry[audio]"   # python-soundfile, so phonometry.io also reads FLAC, AIFF, Ogg/Opus, MP3 and compressed WAV
pip install "phonometry[full]"    # all of the above (recommended)
pip install "phonometry[torch]"   # torch + array-api-compat, so the FDTD engines run on PyTorch (CPU, CUDA or Apple MPS); not part of [full]
```

I recommend `pip install "phonometry[full]"`: it brings matplotlib, numba,
reportlab, svglib and soundfile in one go, so every feature of the library is
enabled except the torch backend of the FDTD engines, which `[torch]` adds. The
base install computes every metric on NumPy and SciPy alone, and that includes
reading every linear measurement WAV (24-bit, multichannel EXTENSIBLE, RF64)
through `phonometry.io`; the only things it leaves unavailable are the figures
(`.plot()` and the filter response plots), the normative PDF fiches
(`.report()`), the compiled kernel that speeds up the `impulse` time weighting,
the other audio formats (FLAC, AIFF, Ogg/Opus, MP3 and compressed WAV), and the
torch backend of the FDTD engines (which run on NumPy either way, and on an
NVIDIA GPU once CuPy is installed). One licensing note on `[audio]`: it
installs python-soundfile, whose wheel bundles **libsndfile under the
LGPL-2.1** (dynamically linked, the same pattern as librosa and torchaudio).
The base install deliberately stays free of it, which is why the metrological
formats need no extra.

One caveat about `[full]`: numba is the only extra that caps NumPy, and it
raises that cap only once it supports a new NumPy minor. So in the weeks after
a NumPy minor release, `phonometry[full]` (like `phonometry[perf]`) can resolve
one minor behind what a plain install gets. numba only makes the `impulse` time
weighting faster, so if you need the newest NumPy the day it ships, install
`phonometry[plot,report]` and leave `[perf]` out.

numba keeps the compiled kernel on disk, beside the package or under your home
directory, so a later process loads it instead of compiling it again. Where
neither is writable, as for a service account or a container user with no home,
it compiles the kernel once in each process instead; point `NUMBA_CACHE_DIR` at
a writable directory to keep it.

`[full]` leaves `[torch]` out on purpose: the torch wheels PyPI serves on Linux
bring gigabytes of CUDA libraries. On a Linux machine without an NVIDIA GPU,
install torch from PyTorch's CPU index first, then the extra, which keeps
the torch it finds, and those libraries stay out:

```bash
pip install torch --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu
pip install "phonometry[torch]"
```

Adding that index with `--extra-index-url` is not enough: pip takes the
newest torch it finds on either index, PyPI's CUDA build included.

[Where a simulation runs](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/simulation/compute/) says what each device of the FDTD engines
needs, CuPy's wheel for an NVIDIA GPU among them, and [pip and
uv](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/environments/pip-and-uv/) what each extra brings and how uv installs
them.

**Option 2: Cloning and Installing**

```bash
git clone https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry.git
```


## The processing chain at a glance

Every phonometry analysis is some subset of one pipeline: take the raw
signal, convert it to physical units, weight it in frequency, split it into
standardized bands, smooth it in time and reduce it to metrics:

## Basic Usage: 1/3 Octave Analysis

Analyze a signal and get the Sound Pressure Level (SPL) per frequency band.

```python
import numpy as np
from phonometry import filters

fs = 48000
t = np.linspace(0, 1, fs, endpoint=False)
# Composite signal: 100Hz + 1000Hz
signal = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 100 * t) + np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1000 * t)

# Apply 1/3 octave filter bank
result = filters.octave_filter(signal, fs=fs, fraction=3)

print(f"Bands: {result.frequencies}")
# Bands: [12.589254117941678, 15.848931924611138, ..., 19952.623149688785]  (33 bands)
print(f"SPL [dB]: {result.levels}")
# SPL [dB]: [46.77640002 47.81719048 48.93133762 ...]
# the 100 Hz band reads about 90.7 dB and the 1 kHz band about 90.9 dB
```

```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import scipy.signal
import numpy as np
from phonometry import filters

fs = 48000
t = np.linspace(0, 1, fs, endpoint=False)
# Composite signal: 100Hz + 1000Hz
signal = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 100 * t) + np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1000 * t)
# Apply 1/3 octave filter bank
filtered = filters.octave_filter(signal, fs=fs, fraction=3)
spl, freq = filtered.levels, filtered.frequencies

# Gray background: the raw-signal PSD (Welch), shifted to sit just below the
# band SPLs so both spectral shapes share one axis.
f_psd, psd = scipy.signal.welch(signal, fs, nperseg=8192)
psd_db = 10 * np.log10(psd + 1e-12)
psd_db += np.max(spl) - np.max(psd_db) - 5

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.semilogx(f_psd, psd_db, color="gray", alpha=0.6, label="Raw signal PSD")
ax.semilogx(freq, spl, marker="o", markerfacecolor="white",
            label="1/3 octave bands")
ax.set_xlabel("Frequency [Hz]")
ax.set_ylabel("SPL [dB]")
ax.legend()
plt.show()
```

## Read a sound level meter WAV

With a real meter you have two WAVs: the calibrator take and the
measurement. `phonometry.io` reads both as measurements: native rate
kept, integer PCM scaled exactly to full scale, channels first, nothing
normalized. The `Signal` it returns carries the sample rate and, once you
attach it, the calibration, so the level functions stop asking you to
repeat either:

```python
import numpy as np
from phonometry import io, metrology, signals

# Both files come from the meter, in this order, through one untouched chain.
cal_take = io.read("calibrator.wav")          # the 94 dB calibrator tone
cal = metrology.sensitivity(np.asarray(cal_take), target_spl=94.0,
                            fs=cal_take.fs)

sig = io.read("measurement.wav", calibration_factor=cal)
print(f"Leq = {float(signals.leq(sig)):.1f} dB")   # no fs, no factor to repeat
```

The base install reads everything a meter writes: 24-bit PCM, multichannel
EXTENSIBLE, overnight RF64. It warns if the file is a compressed listening
copy rather than a linear recording. Write the factor into the sidecar once
(`io.write_sidecar("measurement.wav", cal)`) and from then on
`io.read("measurement.wav")` comes back calibrated with no arguments at all.
[Reading and writing measurement audio](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/io/audio-files/) is the full
workflow: provenance, streaming, BWF writing and lossless conversion.

## Analyzing an audio file

```python
from scipy.io import wavfile
from phonometry import filters

# Load standard WAV file
fs, signal = wavfile.read("measurement.wav")

# Analyze
# Note: To obtain real-world SPL values, you must calibrate the input.
# See the Calibration guide.
filtered = filters.octave_filter(signal, fs=fs, fraction=3)
spl, freq = filtered.levels, filtered.frequencies
```

Integer audio (e.g. int16 WAV data) is converted to float64 internally, so it
is safe to pass `wavfile.read` output directly, but note it is *cast*, not
rescaled to full scale, so keep calibrator and measurement in the same format;
`io.read` in the previous section scales exactly and removes the concern.

## In a notebook

Jupyter and JupyterLab display the value of the last line of a
cell, and a result displays as a table of its fields: each with its unit when
its name carries one (`_db`, `_hz`, `_m`...), names a level or a level
difference (`insertion_loss` and `band_levels` are in decibels) or is an axis
the API names by the bare concept (`frequencies` in hertz, `levels` in decibels, `times` in
seconds), each array as one line with its shape and the range of its values,
never the values themselves, so the frames of a simulation take a single row. A verdict shows its outcome, PASS or FAIL,
in the title. Showing the table reads the fields and, on a verdict, `passes`,
which may read other properties of the verdict; it draws nothing and runs
nothing else. `print()` and `repr()` are unchanged: the dataclass repr, with
numpy's own summary of an array longer than a thousand values.

This cell filters a 1 kHz tone over noise into one-third-octave bands and ends
in the result:

```python
import numpy as np
from phonometry import building, filters

fs = 48000
t = np.arange(fs) / fs
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
noise = np.cumsum(rng.standard_normal(fs))
noise /= np.std(noise)
x = 0.4 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1000.0 * t) + 0.05 * noise

bank = filters.octave_filter(x, fs, fraction=3, limits=[20.0, 20000.0])
bank        # the last line of the cell: the notebook shows its table
```

# Environments

phonometry is pure Python on top of NumPy and SciPy, published as one wheel
for every operating system, and it needs Python 3.12 or newer. The same
package therefore installs wherever those run, from a virtual environment on a
laptop to a container on a server or a notebook in a browser tab. What changes
from one place to the next is how its dependencies arrive and which of the
optional extras can follow them. The pages of this section take one place
each, with commands that were run there.

| Where | How phonometry arrives | What runs | What does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| [pip and uv](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/environments/pip-and-uv/) | `python -m pip install "phonometry[full]"` in a virtual environment, or `uv add "phonometry[full]"` | everything; `[torch]` adds the FDTD engines on PyTorch | nothing is left out |
| [conda and mamba](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/environments/conda/) | the dependencies from conda-forge, then `python -m pip install "phonometry[full]"` | everything, pytorch from conda-forge included | nothing is left out |
| [Jupyter and JupyterLab](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/environments/jupyter/) | `python -m pip install jupyterlab "phonometry[full]"` | record tables, inline figures, fiches, videos once ffmpeg is installed | a kernel started with `PYTHONWARNINGS=error` |
| [JupyterLite and Pyodide](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/environments/jupyterlite/) | `%pip install "phonometry[plot,report,audio]"` in the first cell | metrics, record tables, figures, fiches, WAV and FLAC, FDTD on NumPy | `[perf]`, `[torch]` and `[full]`, video, every compute device but `"cpu"` |

| [Type checking](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/environments/type-checking/) | nothing to add: the wheel ships `py.typed` | `mypy --strict`, and pyright's strict mode from NumPy 2.3 | pyright's strict mode on NumPy 2.0 to 2.2; the FDTD field arrays and the keyword arguments `.plot()` hands to matplotlib, typed `Any` |

## Warnings as errors

Importing phonometry and every one of its domains raises no warning, so a
program, a test suite or a notebook can run with warnings turned into errors
from the first line. Every warning the library itself gives is a
`phonometry.PhonometryWarning`, which lets you make the library's warnings
errors without making errors of every other package's:

```python
import warnings

import numpy as np
import phonometry
from phonometry import filters

fs = 48000
x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1000.0 * np.arange(fs) / fs)
with warnings.catch_warnings():
    warnings.simplefilter("error", phonometry.PhonometryWarning)
    try:
        filters.octave_filter(x, fs, fraction=3, limits=[12.0, 25000.0])
    except filters.FilterBankWarning as warning:
        print(warning)   # Low sampling rate: fs = 48000 Hz cannot hold the 25 kHz band, ...
```

The limits asked for here reach past half the sample rate, which the bank
reports; a call that stays below it raises nothing. That narrower filter is
the one to use in JupyterLite, where the matplotlib Pyodide ships gives a
deprecation warning of its own while it draws, which a blanket
`warnings.simplefilter("error")` turns into an error.

# pip and uv

phonometry installs from PyPI with pip or uv on Python 3.12, 3.13 and 3.14,
as one wheel with no compiled code of its own. Both tools land on the same
packages; the difference is how they keep a project's dependencies, and how
each is told to take torch from PyTorch's CPU index.

## pip in a virtual environment

```bash
python -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
python -m pip install --upgrade pip
python -m pip install "phonometry[full]"
```

`python -m pip install phonometry` alone brings NumPy and SciPy and nothing
else; that is enough for every metric of the library. `python -m pip check`
reports no broken requirement afterwards, with or without extras. Check what
the environment imports:

```python
import phonometry

print(phonometry.__version__)   # the release you installed
```

## What each extra brings

| Extra | Brings | So that |
|---|---|---|
| `[plot]` | matplotlib | `.plot()` and the filter response plots draw |
| `[report]` | reportlab, svglib | `.report()` writes a PDF fiche; its figure panel also needs `[plot]` |
| `[audio]` | python-soundfile, whose wheel carries libsndfile | `phonometry.io` reads FLAC, AIFF, Ogg/Opus, MP3 and compressed WAV, and writes FLAC |
| `[perf]` | numba | the `impulse` time weighting runs compiled |
| `[full]` | all four above | every feature but the torch backend |
| `[torch]` | torch, array-api-compat | the FDTD engines step on PyTorch |

No extra needs a system package: on a bare `python:3.13-slim` image every one
of them installs and runs from its wheels, `[audio]` included. Without numba
the `impulse` time weighting runs as plain Python and says nothing about it.
A feature whose extra is missing says which one to install, with its pip and
its conda spelling:

```text
ImportError: Plotting requires matplotlib. Install it with: pip install "phonometry[plot]" (conda: conda install -c conda-forge matplotlib-base)
```

The oldest release of each dependency the library is tested with, on each
Python, is listed in [Getting
Started](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/getting-started/#installation).

## torch without CUDA

`[full]` leaves `[torch]` out, because the torch wheels PyPI serves on Linux
bring gigabytes of CUDA libraries. On a Linux machine without an NVIDIA GPU,
install torch from PyTorch's CPU index first, then the extra, which keeps the
torch it finds, in the virtual environment made above:

```bash
python -m pip install torch --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu
python -m pip install "phonometry[torch]"
```

The environment then holds the CPU build of torch and no NVIDIA package, and
an FDTD run on `Compute(device="torch:cpu")` gives the same pressures as the
NumPy run. [Where a simulation runs](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/simulation/compute/) covers the
other devices.

## From a clone

```bash
git clone https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry.git
cd phonometry
python -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
python -m pip install --upgrade pip
python -m pip install .
```

pip builds the wheel with setuptools and no compiler, and the build also
passes under `PYTHONWARNINGS=error`.

## uv

`uv pip` takes the same requirements as pip, in an environment uv creates:

```bash
uv venv
uv pip install "phonometry[full]"
```

For torch, `--torch-backend cpu` tells uv to take torch from PyTorch's CPU
index, with no NVIDIA package:

```bash
uv pip install "phonometry[torch]" --torch-backend cpu
```

In a uv project, `uv add` writes the requirement into `pyproject.toml` and the
lock file, and `uv run` runs in the project's environment:

```bash
uv init acoustics
cd acoustics
uv add "phonometry[full]"
uv run python -c "import phonometry; print(phonometry.__version__)"
```

`uv add` resolves the same way in a project for Python 3.12, 3.13 or 3.14.

### torch from the CPU index in a project

Name the index in `pyproject.toml`, mark it explicit so that only torch is
taken from it, and route torch to it:

```toml
[tool.uv.sources]
torch = [{ index = "pytorch-cpu" }]

[[tool.uv.index]]
name = "pytorch-cpu"
url = "https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu"
explicit = true
```

Then add torch by name beside the extra:

```bash
uv add "phonometry[torch]" torch
```

The name matters. A source applies to a dependency the project names itself,
so `uv add "phonometry[torch]"` alone, with the same `pyproject.toml`, locks
the torch of PyPI's default index together with its CUDA and NVIDIA packages,
and says nothing about it.

# conda and mamba

phonometry installs from PyPI. In a conda environment, take its dependencies
from conda-forge first, then install phonometry with the environment's own
pip: pip finds every requirement already there and adds phonometry alone, so
NumPy, SciPy and the rest stay conda-forge builds.

## An environment with every extra

```bash
conda create -n acoustics -c conda-forge python=3.13 numpy scipy matplotlib-base numba reportlab svglib pysoundfile
conda activate acoustics
python -m pip install "phonometry[full]"
```

pip reports each requirement as already satisfied and installs phonometry
only. `conda list` then shows phonometry with the channel `pypi` beside the
conda-forge packages, and `python -m pip check` reports nothing broken. mamba
takes the same arguments: `mamba create -n acoustics -c conda-forge ...`
builds the same environment. Python 3.12, 3.13 and 3.14 all work.

## The extras under their conda-forge names

| Extra | conda-forge packages |
|---|---|
| the base install | numpy, scipy |
| `[plot]` | matplotlib-base |
| `[report]` | reportlab, svglib, and matplotlib-base for the fiche's figure |
| `[audio]` | pysoundfile, which brings libsndfile |
| `[perf]` | numba |
| `[torch]` | pytorch, array-api-compat |

`matplotlib-base` is matplotlib without a GUI backend, which is what the
library draws with; `pysoundfile` is the conda-forge name of the package PyPI
calls `soundfile`. Install the conda-forge packages of the extras you want
before pip runs. A dependency still missing when pip runs comes from PyPI
instead, and `conda list` shows it with the channel `pypi` beside the
conda-forge NumPy.

A feature whose dependency is missing names both spellings:

```text
ImportError: Plotting requires matplotlib. Install it with: pip install "phonometry[plot]" (conda: conda install -c conda-forge matplotlib-base)
```

In a conda environment, the second one is the command to run.

## pytorch

On a machine without a GPU, conda-forge installs its CPU build of pytorch, so
no index has to be named:

```bash
conda create -n fdtd -c conda-forge python=3.14 numpy scipy pytorch array-api-compat
conda activate fdtd
python -m pip install "phonometry[torch]"
```

pip again installs phonometry alone, and an FDTD run on
`Compute(device="torch:cpu")` gives the same pressures as the NumPy run.

# Jupyter and JupyterLab

A notebook runs phonometry in an ordinary IPython kernel, so it installs as
anywhere else; what the notebook adds is how a result shows itself under a
cell. [In a notebook](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/getting-started/#in-a-notebook), in
Getting Started, walks through a first cell and the table it displays; this
page is the rest of what a notebook user meets.

## Installing

```bash
python -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
python -m pip install --upgrade pip
python -m pip install jupyterlab "phonometry[full]"
jupyter lab
```

That one environment holds JupyterLab, the IPython kernel it starts and
phonometry with every extra but `[torch]`.

## A result shows a table

The value on the last line of a cell displays as a table of its fields, with
their units, and the line under it names what the result can draw:

```python
import numpy as np
from phonometry import building, filters, io

fs = 48000
t = np.arange(fs) / fs
x = 0.4 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1000.0 * t)
bands = filters.octave_filter(x, fs, fraction=3)
bands       # the last line of the cell: the notebook shows its table
```

## Figures

`.plot()` draws under the cell through the inline backend, with no
`plt.show()`. The cell also prints the axes it returns, as
`<Axes: title={'center': 'Band levels'}, ...>`; a semicolon after the call
leaves the figure alone. The figures come out as PNG; one line in a cell
switches the session to SVG:

```py
%config InlineBackend.figure_formats = ["svg"]
bands.plot();
```

A `.plot()` that raises, on a misspelt keyword such as `colour=` for instance,
leaves no half-drawn figure under the cell, only the error.

## Fiches

```python
rating = building.weighted_rating(
    [30, 34, 38, 41, 45, 49, 50, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 58, 58, 58]
)
rating.report("rating.pdf")
```

The PDF is written to the path you give, relative to the notebook's working
directory; `[report]` writes it and `[plot]` draws its figure.

## Videos

`io.write_video` and the FDTD animation writers run ffmpeg, a program that
pip does not install. On Debian it comes from the system's packages, with
`apt install ffmpeg`, and [Writing videos](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/io/videos/) gives the
command for conda, macOS and Windows. IPython's own display classes then show
the file under the cell:

```python
from matplotlib.animation import FuncAnimation
from matplotlib.figure import Figure

fig = Figure(figsize=(3.2, 2.4), dpi=100)
ax = fig.add_subplot()
phase = np.linspace(0.0, 2.0 * np.pi, 200)
(line,) = ax.plot(phase, np.sin(phase))


def update(k):
    line.set_ydata(np.sin(phase - 0.2 * k))
    return (line,)


animation = FuncAnimation(fig, update, frames=30, interval=50)
io.write_video("wave.mp4", animation)
io.write_video("wave.gif", animation, video=io.VideoOptions(quality="small"))
```

```py
from IPython.display import Image, Video

Video("wave.mp4", embed=True)   # an MP4 or a WebM, embedded in the notebook
Image(filename="wave.gif")      # a GIF
```

## Warnings in a cell

A warning the library gives names the line of the cell that made the call,
and it shows under every cell that makes one, not only the first:

```text
/tmp/ipykernel_2485/1280619122.py:1: FilterBankWarning: Low sampling rate: fs = 48000 Hz cannot hold the 25 kHz band, whose upper edge 28184 Hz is above fs/2 = 24000 Hz; it was left out of the bank.
  wide = filters.octave_filter(x, fs, fraction=3, limits=[12.0, 25000.0])
```

To have warnings raise instead, set the filter in a cell. All of them:

```py
import warnings

warnings.simplefilter("error")
```

or the library's alone, `warnings.simplefilter("error",
phonometry.PhonometryWarning)`. Do not start the kernel with
`PYTHONWARNINGS=error`: the kernel itself then stops on a DeprecationWarning
of its own event loop before it answers the notebook, and the notebook
reports a kernel that died.

## VS Code and Colab

Both run notebooks on an IPython kernel, so what this page shows should
apply there as well. Neither was tried for this page.

# JupyterLite and Pyodide

JupyterLite runs a notebook in a browser tab, on Pyodide: CPython compiled to
WebAssembly, with nothing to install on the machine. phonometry is pure
Python, so it installs there with micropip and runs on the NumPy, SciPy and
matplotlib that Pyodide builds for the browser. What cannot follow it is
anything that needs a compiled package Pyodide does not build, or a program
of its own.

## Installing in a notebook

```py
%pip install "phonometry[plot,report,audio]"
```

The cell prints nothing when it succeeds. numpy, scipy, matplotlib and
soundfile come from Pyodide's own builds, the last one with libsndfile
compiled for the browser; reportlab and svglib are pure Python and come from
PyPI.

**Name every extra in the first install.** micropip leaves the extras of a
package it already installed alone: once phonometry is there, a later
`%pip install "phonometry[report]"` installs nothing, and prints nothing
either. To add what a later extra would have brought, name its packages:

```py
%pip install reportlab svglib
```

and `%pip install soundfile` for `[audio]`. A message for a missing extra
spells its command as `pip install "phonometry[...]"`; in a JupyterLite cell
it runs with a `%` in front.

## What runs in the browser

Every domain imports, and the record tables, `.plot()` under the cell and
`.report()` work as in Jupyter. WAV and FLAC files are written and read back
in the notebook's own folder. The FDTD engines step on NumPy.

```python
import numpy as np
from phonometry import building, filters, io

fs = 48000
x = 0.4 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1000.0 * np.arange(fs) / fs)
bands = filters.octave_filter(x, fs, fraction=3)
io.write("tone.wav", 0.5 * x, fs)
tone = io.read("tone.wav")
rating = building.weighted_rating(
    [30, 34, 38, 41, 45, 49, 50, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 58, 58, 58]
)
rating.report("rating.pdf")
```

## What does not

- **`[perf]`, `[torch]` and `[full]` do not install.** micropip finds no
  wheel of numba or torch it can run in the browser, and one requirement it
  cannot meet stops the whole install:

  ```text
  ValueError: Can't find a pure Python 3 wheel for 'numba>=0.63.0; python_version >= "3.14" and extra == "perf"'.
  ```

  Without numba, the `impulse` time weighting runs as plain Python and says
  nothing about it.
- **No compute device but `"cpu"`.** `Compute(device="torch:cpu")` raises
  `ComputeDeviceError: device 'torch:cpu' is not available in Pyodide; only
  'cpu' is`; with `fallback=True` the run steps on NumPy and warns
  `ComputeFallbackWarning` instead.
- **No video.** A browser cannot start a program, so `io.write_video` and the
  FDTD animation writers raise `FFmpegNotFoundError: Pyodide cannot start
  programs, so it cannot run ffmpeg; write the video from Python on a
  desktop, a server or a node`.

To make the library's warnings errors in a JupyterLite notebook, filter on
its own class, `warnings.simplefilter("error",
phonometry.PhonometryWarning)`. A blanket `warnings.simplefilter("error")`
also turns into an error a deprecation warning that Pyodide's matplotlib
3.10.8 gives while it draws, and `.plot()` then fails under the cell.

## Pyodide without JupyterLite

In a page or a Node.js script that loads Pyodide itself, micropip is one of
the packages of the Pyodide distribution, which Pyodide does not load until
it is asked to. The script loads it from JavaScript, after `loadPyodide()`:

```js
const pyodide = await loadPyodide();
await pyodide.loadPackage("micropip");
```

and the Python it then hands to `pyodide.runPythonAsync()` installs
phonometry, with the same extras and the same limits:

```py
import micropip

await micropip.install("phonometry[plot,report,audio]")
```

Without the `loadPackage` step, `import micropip` fails with
`ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'micropip'`.

## A JupyterLite site of your own

A course or a lab can publish its notebooks as a JupyterLite site, built once
and served as static files. The build needs `jupyter-server` beside the two
JupyterLite packages, or it stops with "jupyter-server is not installed. You
cannot add custom content to jupyterlite":

```bash
python -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
python -m pip install --upgrade pip
python -m pip install jupyterlite-core jupyterlite-pyodide-kernel jupyter-server
jupyter lite build --contents content --output-dir _output
```

`content` holds the notebooks, and `_output` is the site to serve. The build
also prints two notices, about `libarchive-c` and `jupyterlab_server`, that
change nothing in the site. A wheel placed in a `pypi` folder of the
directory the build runs in is published with the site, and `%pip install`
takes it from there before PyPI.

# Docker and servers

phonometry needs nothing from the operating system beyond Python: every
extra installs from wheels, libsndfile inside the soundfile wheel included,
so a `python:*-slim` image is enough. The one system package worth adding is
ffmpeg, and only for videos.

## An image

```dockerfile
FROM python:3.13-slim
RUN python -m venv /opt/venv \
 && /opt/venv/bin/python -m pip install --no-cache-dir --upgrade pip \
 && /opt/venv/bin/python -m pip install --no-cache-dir "phonometry[full]"
ENV PATH=/opt/venv/bin:$PATH MPLCONFIGDIR=/tmp/matplotlib
COPY analyse.py /app/analyse.py
WORKDIR /tmp
CMD ["python", "/app/analyse.py"]
```

The virtual environment keeps pip's warning about running as root out of the
build log. `analyse.py` stands for your own job; this one writes a band
spectrum, its figure and a fiche:

```python
"""A batch job: a one-third-octave analysis, its figure and a fiche."""
from pathlib import Path

import numpy as np

from phonometry import building, filters

out = Path("out")
out.mkdir(exist_ok=True)

fs = 48000
t = np.arange(2 * fs) / fs
x = 0.1 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1000 * t)

bands = filters.octave_filter(x, fs, fraction=3)
bands.plot().figure.savefig(out / "bands.png")
impulse = filters.time_weighting(x, fs, mode="impulse")
rating = building.weighted_rating(
    [30, 34, 38, 41, 45, 49, 50, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 58, 58, 58]
)
rating.report(out / "rating.pdf")
print(f"{len(bands.frequencies)} bands, impulse maximum {float(np.max(impulse)):.4f}")
print("wrote", sorted(p.name for p in out.iterdir()))
```

```bash
docker build -t acoustics .
mkdir -p out
docker run --rm --user "$(id -u):$(id -g)" -v "$PWD/out:/tmp/out" acoustics
```

```text
33 bands, impulse maximum 0.0092
wrote ['bands.png', 'rating.pdf']
```

The job writes into `out` under its working directory, `/tmp/out` in the
container, and `--rm` deletes the container with its files when the job ends.
The `-v` mount gives that folder to the host as `out` in the directory you run
from, where the figure and the fiche stay; `--user` runs the job as you, so
the files are yours and not root's. Make the folder first: one that Docker
makes for the mount belongs to root, and the job, running as you, cannot
write in it.

matplotlib picks its Agg backend by itself in a container with no display,
so nothing has to set `MPLBACKEND`.

## Any user, any file system

The image runs the same job as root, as an arbitrary user id
(`docker run --user 12345:12345`), as `nobody`, and on a read-only root file
system with a writable `/tmp` (`docker run --read-only --tmpfs /tmp`). A
folder mounted for the results has to be writable by the user the job runs
as, as `out` is for you above. Two caches and one directory decide how
quietly it does so:

- **`MPLCONFIGDIR`.** matplotlib keeps its configuration and cache under the
  user's home. For a user with no writable home it prints "Matplotlib created
  a temporary cache directory at ..." each time it starts; the `ENV` line
  above points it at `/tmp` instead, and it says nothing.
- **numba's cache.** numba keeps the compiled `impulse` kernel beside the
  package or under the home directory. Where it can write to neither, it
  compiles the kernel once in each process instead and says nothing. With
  `NUMBA_CACHE_DIR=/tmp/numba` it writes the kernel there, for the processes
  that follow in the same container.
- **A read-only root.** The job needs somewhere to write: here its working
  directory, `/tmp`, which `--tmpfs /tmp` keeps writable, and the `out`
  folder mounted inside it from the host. With no writable mount at all, the
  job stops at its own `out.mkdir`.

## Videos

`io.write_video` and the FDTD animation writers run ffmpeg. Install it from
the image's own packages before the virtual environment:

```dockerfile
FROM python:3.13-slim
RUN apt-get update \
 && apt-get install -y --no-install-recommends ffmpeg \
 && rm -rf /var/lib/apt/lists/*
RUN python -m venv /opt/venv \
 && /opt/venv/bin/python -m pip install --no-cache-dir --upgrade pip \
 && /opt/venv/bin/python -m pip install --no-cache-dir "phonometry[full]"
ENV PATH=/opt/venv/bin:$PATH MPLCONFIGDIR=/tmp/matplotlib
```

WebM and MP4 then encode on the CPU, as `nobody` and on a read-only root
with a writable `/tmp` alike. [Writing videos](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/io/videos/) covers
the encoders and a node that has a GPU.

## Servers and threads

A server that handles requests in a thread pool may import the domains it
needs from those threads, several at once. [Sending a simulation to a remote
node](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/simulation/remote-node/) covers an FDTD run the server
should not step itself.

# Type checking

phonometry ships its types in the wheel, with the `py.typed` marker that tells
a type checker to read them, so mypy and pyright check a call into the
library with nothing else installed. Every public module declares what it
publishes, and the lazy domains of `import phonometry as ph` are visible to a
checker as well as at run time.

## A script under the strict modes

```python
import numpy as np

from phonometry import filters, io, signals, simulation

fs = 48000
x = 0.1 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 1000.0 * np.arange(fs) / fs)

bands = filters.octave_filter(x, fs, fraction=3)
levels = bands.require_levels()          # an array, where bands.levels may be None

recording = io.Signal(x, fs)
weighted = filters.WeightingFilter(fs, "A").filter(recording)   # a Signal in, a Signal out
level = float(signals.leq(weighted))      # leq returns a float, or an array for several channels
print(f"Leq = {level:.1f} dB over {levels.size} bands")

engine = simulation.FDTD2D(c=343.0, dx=0.01, shape=(64, 64))
pressure = engine.numpy_field("p")       # a NumPy array; engine.p is the device's own
print(pressure.shape)
```

`mypy --strict` and pyright's basic mode report nothing on this script, on
Python 3.12 to 3.14 and with every NumPy the library accepts there. pyright's
strict mode reports nothing either from NumPy 2.3 on, the oldest the library
accepts on Python 3.14. With NumPy 2.0 to 2.2, which Python 3.12 and 3.13
still accept, `np.ndarray` has no default type arguments, so strict pyright
reads the arrays phonometry annotates as partially unknown: three errors on
this script, from `require_levels()` and `filter()`, and two more from
`numpy_field()` before NumPy 2.2.

## What a checker catches

A text where the library takes numbers, a choice outside the ones a
parameter accepts, a write into a published table and a field a catalogue
row does not have are all refused before the code runs. In `wrong.py`:

```py
from phonometry import filters, materials, signals

signals.leq("not a signal")
filters.WeightingFilter(48000, "Q")
row = next(iter(materials.PUBLISHED_ABSORPTION.values()))
materials.PUBLISHED_ABSORPTION["mine"] = row
print(row.density)
```

`mypy wrong.py` says:

```text
wrong.py:3: error: Argument 1 to "leq" has incompatible type "str"; expected "Signal | complex | NestedSequence[complex] | SupportsNumericArray | NestedSequence[SupportsNumericArray]"  [arg-type]
wrong.py:4: error: Argument 2 to "WeightingFilter" has incompatible type "Literal['Q']"; expected "Literal['A', 'C', 'B', 'D', 'G', 'AU', '468', 'Z']"  [arg-type]
wrong.py:6: error: Unsupported target for indexed assignment ("Mapping[str, AbsorptionSpectrum]")  [index]
wrong.py:7: error: "AbsorptionSpectrum" has no attribute "density"  [attr-defined]
Found 4 errors in 1 file (checked 1 source file)
```

and pyright refuses the same four lines.

## Where the types are wider than the value

- **A field that may be absent.** `bands.levels` is `None` for a call that
  asked for no level, so its type says `np.ndarray | None`; the result's
  `require_levels()` returns the array, or raises a ValueError that says why
  there is none. `require_bands()` does the same for the per-band waveforms.
- **The type a filter hands back.** `WeightingFilter.filter()` and
  `ParametricEQ.filter()` return a `Signal` for a `Signal` and an array for
  an array, and the checker sees which.
- **The FDTD fields.** `engine.p`, `engine.vx` and the other fields an FDTD
  engine steps are arrays of the library it steps on: a NumPy array, a CuPy
  array or a PyTorch tensor. A checker reads them as `Any`.
  `engine.numpy_field("p")` returns a NumPy copy on every device, typed as
  one.
- **The keyword arguments of a plot.** `.plot()` hands the keyword arguments
  it does not take itself on to matplotlib, typed `Any`, so a checker passes
  a misspelt one such as `bands.plot(colour="red")`, which matplotlib refuses
  with an AttributeError when the line runs.

## Completeness

`pyright --verifytypes phonometry --ignoreexternal` reads the installed
package as a user's checker does and scores it at 100% with NumPy 2.3 or
newer: every symbol the package exports has a known type. With NumPy 2.0 to
2.2 it scores about 86%, for the reason the strict mode reports above. The
repository's CI runs the same command on the built wheel, with the newest
NumPy, and fails below 100%.

# Why phonometry

phonometry is a standards-based acoustic measurement toolkit. Its
differentiator is not the list of features but how they are built: every
metric is implemented from the governing standard's text, and the standard's
own reference values and acceptance limits are transcribed into the test
suite and enforced in CI. This page explains that approach with a concrete
case study (time weighting under **IEC 61672-1:2013**) and summarizes what
is conformance-tested today. The time-weighting analysis was originally
published in [issue #38](https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/issues/38).

## Design philosophy: a case study in time weighting

Standard time weighting is defined as a **continuous function of time** via the
differential equation

$$
\tau \frac{dy(t)}{dt} + y(t) = x^2(t)
$$

which corresponds to a stable first-order low-pass filter with a pole in the
left half-plane ($s = -1/\tau$). It is worth setting that detector beside the
other thing a Fast reading is sometimes computed with, an energy average
restarted every $\tau$ seconds, because the two are easy to confuse and answer
different questions:

| | Exponential detector (IEC 61672-1) | Block integrator |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Output | Continuous time-weighted envelope, one value per sample | Stepped, one value per block |
| Mechanism | Stable exponential averaging, pole at $s=-1/\tau$ | An energy average restarted every $\tau$ seconds; $\tau$ is in its clock, not in its response |
| What it measures | The standard's Fast/Slow/Impulse level | $L_{\mathrm{eq},\tau}$, energy per interval |

A pole on the negative real axis corresponds to a decaying exponential impulse
response ($h(t) \propto \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}$), exactly what "exponential time
weighting" means: past events are forgotten exponentially. An implementation
that instead accumulates energy and resets every $\tau$ seconds has built
the block integrator, whatever it is called, and one that puts the pole on
the positive real axis before the same reset adds a growing weight inside
each block on top: neither is the time weighting, because the reset changes
the measurement's nature.

That equation is also where the reference numbers of the next section come
from, which is worth doing once rather than taking Table 4 on faith. Integrate
it from rest over a burst of duration $T_\mathrm{b}$ and the envelope reaches
$(1-\mathrm{e}^{-T_\mathrm{b}/\tau})$ of the steady value the same tone would eventually
produce, so the maximum time-weighted level relative to steady state is

$$
\delta_{\text{ref}} = 10\lg\!\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-T_\mathrm{b}/\tau}\right)
$$

which is IEC 61672-1:2013 Equation (7). With $\tau_\mathrm{F} = 0.125$ s that
gives −0.98 dB at 200 ms, −4.82 dB at 50 ms, −11.14 dB at 10 ms and −20.99 dB
at 1 ms: the whole "IEC target" column of Table 4, to the standard's own
rounding. CI asserts the identity for every Fast and Slow row
(`test_delta_ref_equation7_consistency`, to within 0.15 dB), so the
transcription of the table is itself checked rather than trusted.

The consequence carries the rest of this page. Because the response is a smooth
function of $T_\mathrm{b}/\tau$, a detector that really solves the equation
tracks the whole column at once; a detector that averages over fixed
125 ms blocks answers with the burst's energy share of whichever blocks it
lands in, which moves with duration and alignment alike but carries nothing
of the $T_\mathrm{b}/\tau$ response, and it can only be right where the burst
happens to fill a block.

## Verification against IEC 61672-1 (tone bursts)

The rigorous test for time weighting is the **Tone Burst Response**
(IEC 61672-1, Table 4), using a 4 kHz sine burst referenced to the steady-state
level.

### How the check is run

Table 4 fixes the excitation but not the plumbing, so here is the plumbing. A
4 kHz sine is generated at 48 kHz for 2 s. The **reference** is the steady Fast
level of that continuous sine, averaged over its last half second. Once the
integrator has settled, this is $L_\mathrm{A}$ in the standard's notation. The
burst of the stated duration is then cut out of *the same* sine, so its phase
and amplitude are identical to the reference tone's, and zeros surround it. The
**response** is the maximum of the Fast envelope of that burst, expressed
relative to the reference: $10\log_{10}(\max \text{env} / \text{ref})$, which
is the standard's $L_\mathrm{AFmax} - L_\mathrm{A}$. Note what this does and
does not verify: IEC 61672-1 states these limits for the electrical input
facility over a defined range of steady levels with no overload indicated
(clauses 5.9.5-5.9.6), so the numbers below verify the *ballistics*, not a
complete instrument. The same reference values are transcribed in
`tests/filters/test_iec_compliance.py` for F and S and for the
$L_{\mathrm{A}E}$ column.

**phonometry results (Fast weighting):**

| Burst duration | IEC target (dB) | Class 1 limit (dB) | phonometry (dB) | Error (dB) | Status |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 200 ms | −1.0 | ±0.5 | −0.98 | +0.02 | Pass |
| 50 ms | −4.8 | ±1.0 | −4.82 | −0.02 | Pass |
| 10 ms | −11.1 | ±1.0 | −11.14 | −0.04 | Pass |
| 1 ms | −21.0 | +1.0 / −2.0 | −20.99 | +0.01 | Pass |

The exponential detector stays within 0.05 dB of the Table 4 reference at
every duration, and no row spends more than 4 % of its own class 1
allowance, because a detector that
solves the defining equation has no free parameter left to get wrong. A block
integrator can also sit inside class 1 at a favourable alignment, but what it
spends of the budget is set by how the burst happens to straddle a 125 ms
block, which is luck rather than design. The first column of Table 4
tightens to ±0.5 dB for bursts of 200 ms and longer, where there is little
margin left to lose.

The generator behind this figure is
`scripts/figures/signals.py::generate_tone_burst_iec`, and it is the procedure

The figure fixes the burst at one place in time, which is the one thing the
block integrator's answer depends on and the exponential detector's does not.
Slide the same burst across a 125 ms block boundary and the two behave quite
differently: the exponential reading is pinned at −4.82 dB whatever the
alignment, a spread of 0.001 dB, while the block reading swings over 2.94 dB
and spends 12 % of the alignments outside the class 1 corridor. At 200 ms,
where Table 4 tightens to ±0.5 dB, the block integrator is outside the
corridor for 81 % of them, because a 200 ms burst always fills at least part
of a block and often fills one completely, and a full block reads the steady
level, 1.0 dB above the target.

*The exponential trace is flat because $10\lg(1-\mathrm{e}^{-T_\mathrm{b}/\tau})$
contains $T_\mathrm{b}$ and nothing about the clock; the block trace, at this fixed
duration, is a picture of the alignment and nothing else. Both readings are computed here
with `time_weighting(x, fs, mode="fast")` and with `leq` over consecutive
125 ms slices, against the same steady-tone reference the procedure above
defines.*

## What this means in practice

- If you need **standard-compliant Fast/Slow/Impulse envelopes** (sound level
  meter behavior, one level per sample), use phonometry's
  [`time_weighting`](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/levels/time-weighting/).
- If you need **block-averaged $L_\mathrm{eq}$ per interval**, that is a different, equally
  valid metric; you can compute it with [`leq`](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/levels/levels/) over consecutive
  slices.
- Both approaches are useful; they just answer different questions. The
  discrepancy reported in issue #38 comes from comparing a continuous envelope
  against a block integrator, not from an implementation error.

## Conformance testing across the library

A word on what a **performance class** is, since every verdict on this page is
one. In IEC 61672-1 and IEC 61260-1 a class is not a grade of accuracy but a
named tolerance corridor around a nominal response: class 1 is the narrow
corridor intended for precision work, class 2 the wider one for general survey
use, and both widen at the extremes of the frequency range, where they are
hardest to meet. Class 0 was a narrower corridor still, in the withdrawn 1995
edition of IEC 61260, and is kept here as a voluntary extra target for the
default Butterworth bank. So a pass on this page means the computed response
never leaves the corridor the standard draws; it does not mean the error is
zero, and the size of the margin is the interesting number.

The tone-burst case above is not an isolated check. For each standard the
library implements, the reference values and acceptance limits are transcribed
from the official text into the test suite, so any regression fails CI. A
sample from the metrology core:

The same discipline applies far beyond the metrology core: today the suite runs
1897 numerical conformance checks across 111 domains, against
227 normative designations and 125 further published sources, covering
psychoacoustics and speech intelligibility, room, building and materials
acoustics, human and machine vibration, environmental, aircraft, rotorcraft
and underwater noise, electroacoustics, broadcast loudness, industrial noise
control, calibrated signal analysis and the FDTD wave solver. The full
numerical report (the expected value and the value the library computes for
every check, regenerated on every pull request) is published as
[CONFORMANCE.md](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/conformance/).

The same standards-first habit shows up below the level of whole metrics, in
the numerics. Filter banks place their −3 dB points on the **ANSI S1.11 /
IEC 61260-1** band edges for the three architectures whose parametrization
allows it, Butterworth, Chebyshev II and Bessel, the last two through
corrections scipy's raw parametrization would not apply; Chebyshev I and
elliptic read the same edges as their equiripple passband edge instead, which
is stated with its measured cost on
[Filter Banks](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/filters/filter-banks/). The default Butterworth
bank is checked against the stricter class 0 of the withdrawn IEC 61260:1995 /
ANSI S1.11-2004 edition as well as the current class 1. And A/C weighting
earns class 1 at each of the eight rates the suite grades it at, from 8 to
192 kHz, at every Table 3 frequency below that rate's Nyquist, because the
analog prototype is fitted at the sample rate rather than transformed blind (see
[Frequency Weighting](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/signals/levels/weighting/)).

## When the source is what is wrong

Transcribing a standard rather than porting somebody else's code has a
consequence that only shows up at scale: sooner or later the recomputed value
and the printed one disagree, and sometimes it is the printed one that cannot
be right. A worked example that contradicts its own normative clause, a
constant with a digit lost in typesetting, a cross-reference pointing at the
wrong equation.

Those cases are not quietly patched. Each confirmed one is written down in
[Standards errata](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/errata/) with the printed edition and the exact location,
what the document says, why it cannot be right, the independent evidence and
the reading the library implements. Where that reading changes a number the
library reports, the entry names the check or test that pins it; where it does
not, because the defect is a label, a cross-reference or a table the library
never reads, the entry says so instead.
There are dozens of them now, across standards, guidance documents, textbooks
and journal papers, each marked as reported to the issuing body or not. A
defect listed there is never a defect of the method: in every case the intended
reading could be established from the document itself or from physics.

That registry is the part of the approach that is hardest to fake. Code ported
from another implementation inherits whatever the misprint made it do, and
nothing in the port ever notices.

## Where phonometry fits in the Python ecosystem

Several Python projects share ground with phonometry, and the honest way to
place them is by what each is built for rather than by racing them:

- **acoustic-toolbox** is the community successor to python-acoustics, which
  was archived in February 2024. It is a general acoustics toolkit, and its
  IEC 61672-1 time and frequency weighting builds on `pyoctaveband`, the
  former name of this library, which since 2.1.0 is a shim over phonometry.
- **MoSQITo** is the reference open implementation for psychoacoustic
  sound-quality metrics: Zwicker loudness, ECMA-418-2 loudness and roughness,
  DIN 45692 sharpness, ECMA-74 tone-to-noise and prominence ratio, the
  ANSI S3.5 speech intelligibility index. phonometry implements the same
  family, adds ISO 532-2 and ISO 532-3 loudness, ECMA-418-2 tonality,
  fluctuation strength and the Fastl & Zwicker annoyance model, and sits them
  in the same conformance harness as the metrology core, composed directly
  with the weighting filters, ballistics, band filtering and calibration that
  feed them.
- **pyroomacoustics** is built for simulating rooms in audio and machine
  learning pipelines: image sources, ray tracing, beamforming, direction of
  arrival, plus sweep-based impulse-response acquisition. phonometry's room
  side is the measurement one: its image-source and FDTD solvers are pinned
  to published closed forms, and a simulated impulse response goes through
  the same `room_parameters` analysis (EDT, T20, T30, C50, C80, D50, Ts, per
  band, with the ISO 3382 decay-range validity flags) as a measured one.

If your work needs numbers you can defend against a standard's tolerance
table, whether for measurement reports, environmental assessments or
instrument cross-checks, that verification layer is what phonometry is for.
Where the standard also fixes how the result is to be reported, the result
object renders that one-page fiche itself with `.report()`, in English or
Spanish, so the document you hand over carries the same numbers the check does.
The sources behind all of it are collected in the
[Bibliography](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/bibliography/), every entry with a verified DOI or official
publisher link.

## How the library is built

The method is the whole point of the project, and it is deliberately narrow.

1. **Each metric is implemented from the text of its governing standard**,
   clause by clause, rather than from a secondary description of it or from
   somebody else's implementation. Where a formula is ambiguous, the ambiguity
   is resolved from the document itself or from the physics, and the reading
   chosen is written down.
2. **The standard's own reference values are transcribed into the test
   suite.** Where a standard publishes a worked example, a tolerance table or a
   set of nominal responses (the IEC 61672-1 Table 3 weighting values and its
   Table 4 tone-burst responses, the IEC 61260-1 Table 1 filter limits, the
   ISO 226 Annex B contours, and so on): those numbers become the expected
   values of real tests.
3. **CI enforces them on every change.** A regression that moves a computed
   value outside the standard's acceptance limit fails the build, so a claim
   cannot quietly stop being true between releases.

Not every standard publishes a numerical example. Where one does not, the
implementation is anchored to the closed-form expressions of the normative text
and pinned with a case synthesized to a known result. That is a weaker
guarantee than a transcribed worked example, and the
[conformance report](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/conformance/) says so in the expected-value cell
itself rather than as a flag you could filter away: look for the words "closed
form" or "analytic". The distinction is worth stating plainly. A closed-form
check proves the implementation agrees with the equations as written; it cannot
catch a misreading of the clause those equations came from. That is why the
[errata registry](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/errata/) exists beside it.

The evidence is public. The conformance report lists, per check, the standard
and clause, the normative expected value, the value the library computes, the
delta and a pass or fail verdict, and CI regenerates it on every pull request
so it cannot drift from the code. [Why phonometry](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/start/why-phonometry/) walks the
method end to end on a single case (time weighting under IEC 61672-1:2013)
if you want to see it applied before trusting it in general.

## Reporting an error

Please report it. A wrong number nobody mentions stays wrong.

Open an issue at
[github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/issues](https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/issues).
The most useful report names the standard and clause you are checking against,
the expected value, the value phonometry produced, and a short snippet that
reproduces it. If the signal cannot be shared, the parameters alone are usually
enough to rebuild a case.

If an issue tracker is not an option, write to [mail@jmrp.io](mailto:mail@jmrp.io);
the issue is still the better channel, because the report and the fix stay
public next to the code they concern.

**That includes errors in the standards themselves.** Re-deriving formulae and
recomputing worked examples from the source documents occasionally turns up
defects in the sources: misprints, worked examples that contradict their own
normative clauses, ambiguous wording. Those are not silently worked around.
Each confirmed case is recorded in [`ERRATA.md`](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/errata/) with the
location, the evidence, the reading the library implements and whether it has
been reported to the issuing body; the site transplants that same file at build
time, so the two editions cannot disagree. The registry covers standards
(ISO, IEC, EN), guidance documents and technical reports, textbooks and journal
papers alike. Disagreeing with one of those readings is exactly the kind of
issue worth opening.

**[doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21215280](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21215280)**

That is the *concept* DOI: it always resolves to the most recent archived
release, and each release also carries its own version DOI on the same record.
Cite the version you actually ran.

APA:

> Requena-Plens, J. M. (2026). *phonometry: acoustic measurement, analysis and
> prediction for Python (formerly PyOctaveBand)* (Version 4.0.0rc1)
> [Computer software]. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21215280

BibTeX:

```bibtex
@software{requenaplens_phonometry,
  author  = {Requena-Plens, Jos{\'e} M.},

             Python (formerly PyOctaveBand)},
  year    = {2026},

  doi     = {10.5281/zenodo.21215280},
  url     = {https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/},

```

Both entries derive from
[`CITATION.cff`](https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/blob/main/CITATION.cff)
in the repository, which is the authoritative metadata and the file GitHub and
Zenodo read. With a reference manager, import that file rather than copying the
block above, and adjust the version and year to the release you used.

## Licence

phonometry is released under the
[MIT licence](https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/blob/main/LICENSE), so it
can be used in commercial and academic work, modified and redistributed, as
long as the copyright notice and the licence text travel with it. Data bundled
from a third party keeps its source's terms: the EASA Aircraft Noise and
Performance (ANP) tables, for one, are reproduced under EASA's notice that
authorises reproduction provided the source is acknowledged, as their
[provenance note](https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/blob/main/src/phonometry/aircraft/data/anp/PROVENANCE.md)
records. The software comes without warranty: it is verified against the
standards it implements, as described above, but **it is not a calibrated
instrument and it carries no accreditation**. Deciding whether a result is fit
for a purpose stays with the person using it.

Concretely, for the laboratories and consultancies this is mostly written for:
the library computes and documents, it does not measure, so **traceability
stays with the hardware chain**: a calibrator and a microphone with valid
certificates, and type-approved instrumentation wherever the measurement has
legal effect. Accreditation bodies expect **calculation software to be
validated for its intended use, with the record retained**, and the artefact
for that is the conformance report of the exact version that ran: pin the
version, archive its report with the measurement data, and keep the errata
registry alongside, since that is where every difference between the
implemented reading and a printed source is documented. The uncertainty
machinery propagates what it is given: the calibrator tolerance and the
pre/post drift are still yours to enter.

The standards themselves are copyrighted by their issuing bodies and are not
redistributed here. This documentation cites clauses and reference values as
far as it takes to explain and verify the implementations; it is not a
substitute for buying the documents.

# Support the project

phonometry is maintained by one person, and its real cost is not hosting or
tooling: it is the primary sources. Every metric in the library is implemented
from the governing standard's own text, and standards are paid documents that
go out of print, get superseded and occasionally resist every legitimate
acquisition route there is. This page lists the ways to help, from free to
concrete.

## Use it, and tell me when a number looks wrong

The cheapest support is using the library and reporting the number that does
not match your reference. A report that names a standard and a clause is the
most valuable issue this project receives: the
[errata register](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/errata/) exists because sources get read closely, and
several of its entries began as a user's raised eyebrow.
[Open an issue](https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/issues) with what you
measured and what you expected.

## Sponsor

[GitHub Sponsors](https://github.com/sponsors/jmrplens) and
[Ko-fi](https://ko-fi.com/jmrplens) both fund the project directly, and the
money goes where the cost is: buying the standards and editions the
implementations are written from. A one-off sponsorship or Ko-fi donation
that names one of the documents below buys exactly that document, and the
entry leaves the table when it lands.

## The sources I could not obtain

Everything below stands between the library and work it would otherwise do.
Each entry has survived the whole acquisition effort: my own library, the
official stores, the open-access indexes and every other legitimate channel I
could find, re-checked on the date shown. Four routes help:

- **Fund the purchase.** A one-off sponsorship or Ko-fi donation naming the
  document; where a link goes to an official store, the price is on that
  page, and for the rest name the item and I will quote it in the issue.
- **Send me a copy.** Whatever its origin. It stays private, it is used only
  to implement and to verify, and it is never republished: the repository
  only ever carries the derived numeric values. Write to
  [mail@jmrp.io](mailto:mail@jmrp.io).
- **Verify against your licensed copy.** If your lab or employer already
  holds one, you can check the implementation and its reference values
  against your copy. What lands in the tree are the derived numeric values,
  which is how the [conformance suite](https://docs.jmrp.io/phonometry/reference/conformance/) is built.
- **Point me at a channel I missed.** A lending library, an author's own
  copy of a thesis, a proceedings archive that actually resolves.
  [Open an issue](https://github.com/jmrplens/phonometry/issues).
